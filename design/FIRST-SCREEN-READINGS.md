## Round 1 (option A at commit 6056384 and later fixes to fit; B the same, 11-07)

### A, the reader with a broad education and no statistics

> **What the page claims.** It asks how much a better election result would need to be worth to me, in dollars, before casting one vote is worth the bother. My answer from the page: in the closest presidential states in 2008, about $89 million. I assume my vote has a tiny chance of changing the winner, so only a very big prize makes the effort pay.
>
> **The numbers.**
> - **$89 million "to you".** At first I thought this was money I'd receive. Then I saw it's the break-even point: how much I'd have to value the outcome.
> - **$8.87 "Your cost".** I guess it's the price of voting, but the page never says what it covers (time, travel?).
> - **"1 in 10 million".** The chance my ballot decides the winner.
> - **$8.87 × 10,000,000.** This works out to about $89 million. If the odds are 1 in 10 million, the prize must be 10 million times my cost to break even.
> - **Grey dots.** The banner says they're invented sample contests. Each row is a type of race, the number on the right is how many contests, and one dot stands for 100. The scale along the bottom runs from 1¢ to $1 trillion. The blue line is my $8.87. The ringed dots are probably the named figures, though that's a guess.
> - **Named figures.** They're real, sourced values for different races.
> - **After "To each person".** It shows 30¢, which is $89 million ÷ 300 million people. I read it as the outcome being worth 30 cents to every American. That's the same total spread thinly, but I'm not sure why it's useful.
>
> **Why the numbers differ.** The page doesn't say, so I'm inferring. The smaller the chance my vote decides things, the bigger the prize must be. In the closest states my vote is likelier to matter, so the figure is $89 million. In the safest states it's nearly hopeless, so it's $8.9 billion. In a town of 2,000 voters my vote matters far more, so it's only $4,150. The House at $1.6 million sits in between. I'm unsure why "President, average voter" is $530 million, or why the House figure covers 1898–1992 while the others are 2008.
>
> **What I couldn't follow or got wrong.**
> - I first misread "to you" as a payment, and 30¢ as money handed out.
> - The $8.87 and the 1-in-10-million odds have no source or explanation. That worries me, since the banner promises sourced figures.
> - After the tap, the town race falls from $4,150 to $1.04. That implies about 4,000 people, not 2,000 voters. The House figure falls to $2.28, which implies roughly 700,000 people. The page never states these divisors.
> - "A sample population" and "sample contests" left me unsure which parts are real.
> - The bottom scale has unexplained steps.

### A, the total layperson

> 1. What the page claims. Told to a friend: one vote almost never decides an election, so for it to be worth the bother, the result would have to be worth a huge amount of money to you. The example is the 2008 presidential race in four close states, and the amount is $89 million. The page never says "so don't vote" or "so do". It says every other reason to vote is "set aside" without telling me what those reasons are.
>
> 2. The numbers.
> - $89 million "to you": I first read it as something I'd win. I think it's how much the outcome would have to be worth to me. I don't have anything like that, so it felt unreal.
> - $8.87 "your cost": I guess the cost of voting, such as time or travel. It doesn't say what's in it.
> - 1 in 10 million: my chance of being the vote that changes the winner. It feels like "basically never".
> - $8.87 × 10,000,000: I did the sum. It's about $88.7 million, so the $89 million. The page doesn't say why you multiply. My guess is that a tiny chance means the prize must be ten million times the cost.
> - Grey dots: the top banner says they're invented samples. Each row is a kind of race. The number at the right (663, 10,875) is how many races, and a dot is 100 of them. Left to right is money, from 1 cent up to $1T, which I assume is trillion. The circled dots are the real named ones.
> - Named list: the same sort of figure for particular cases. Sources are named at the bottom.
> - After tapping "To each person": 30¢, which is $89 million shared across 300 million people. The other figures shrank too. $4,150 became $1.04 and $1.6 million became $2.28. I couldn't see what each was divided by. Is 30¢ small compared with my $8.87? I don't know what I'm meant to conclude.
>
> 3. Why the races differ. This is my guess from the order of the list. A small town race with 2,000 voters needs only $4,150, because my vote is a bigger slice. The House needs $1.6 million. For president, the close states need $89 million and the average voter $530 million. The "safest states" need $8.9 billion, which I take to mean one side is sure to win, so my vote matters least. The page never says this.
>
> 4. What lost me.
> - What "to you" means when I'm not worth $89 million.
> - Whether "pay" means paying money.
> - Why there are "663" presidential contests by state when there are only about 50 states.
> - What "stylised" means. I took it as pretend.
> - Why a 2026 page uses 2008.
> - Why the chance stays 1 in 10 million while the dots show other races.
> - Whether anything I'm looking at is real, since real and invented figures are mixed.

### B, the reader with a broad education and no statistics

So it asks how much you'd have to care about the result for the trip to be worth making. For the presidency in a close state that's $89 million, which seems absurd. >
> **2. The numbers.**
> - **$8.87** is "your cost". It doesn't say what it's made of, so I assumed time and effort.
> - **1 in 10 million** is the chance my ballot changes the winner.
> - **$8.87 × 10,000,000 = $89 million.** At those odds, the outcome would have to be worth that much to me before one vote pays for itself. I first read $89 million as something I'd receive.

> - I can't picture $130 million to me, so I don't know where to put the circle.
> - Several figures have dotted underlines, and I didn't know they could be tapped.
