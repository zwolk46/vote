# The presidency, 1976 to 2024: sources, method and checks

*Written 2026-11-07 by worker D1 (`briefs/D1-data-president.md`). Every figure below is read from the committed files named, which `pipeline/president.py` and `pipeline/own_forecast.py` write; rerunning them reproduces each one. Rulings cited by time are the orchestrator's, of 2026-11-07 (UTC).*

## What is here

`data/president/<year>.json` holds one file per election, 1976 to 2024, in the layout of plan §3. Every field added to that layout is listed in `data/README.md`. Each file has 51 contests (the states and DC), and from 2008 one more for each Maine and Nebraska congressional district (56 contests). Each contest carries:

- its certified result, with the second source's figures and every difference;
- the population of the state and of the nation;
- its electoral votes;
- every forecast read, each with its own p where one can be computed;
- the p shown first, with both of its stages;
- notes on anything ex post or missing.

**p** is the chance that this ballot changes who becomes President. A tie is counted as half (a fair lot). The **event**, twice p, is the chance that the count without the ballot lands within one vote.

## Method

### p in two stages

p = p_state × W (model §3; MATH §4).

- **p_state** is the chance that the unit's count without the ballot is tied, our convention. It is computed as f(½) / (2n), the density form, which `core.p_contest`'s own rule chooses at these n (n ≥ 1000 and n·sd² ≥ 6.25).
  - f is the forecast density of the Democrat's two-party share.
  - n is the two nominees' certified votes less one. It is ex post, since no voter knew it, as the model allows: the count fixes the scale, the forecast the closeness. Every contest says so in its notes.
- **W** is the chance that the unit's electoral votes decide the College, *given that the unit is tied*. It comes from a joint forecast, never from the independence shortcut.
  - The unit decides when the other units give the Democrat between 270 − e and 268 votes. Each endpoint where a 269–269 tie would result is weighted ½: the House's choice is counted as a fair lot.
  - With a common national swing, conditioning on the tie moves the other units, which the shortcut ignores. `W_unconditional`, the shortcut's figure, is recorded for comparison only. In 2024 the conditional W is 1.2 to 1.35 times the unconditional in each close state (PA 0.186 against 0.140; NV 0.051 against 0.038; NC 1.17 times). In California it is half (0.148 against 0.289). Our reading: a tie in California means a national swing toward Trump so large that the other states rarely leave the College close.
- **The College stage** (`pipeline/college.py`) has two forms:
  - Under a swing model: a common national error, plus each unit's own independent error. The posterior of the national error given the unit's tie is put on a grid over ±10 sds (401 points). At each point, a dynamic programme over the other units' electoral votes gives the chance the unit decides. With normal errors this equals the closed form exactly (tested to 10⁻⁶ in `pipeline/test_college.py`).
  - Under the Economist: its own draws (below).

### Which forecast in which year

These are the orchestrator's rulings of 03:51 (under the maker's word of 02:38) and of 04:12 and 04:20.

- **1984–2004 and 2024:** the model's own forecast.
- **2008 and 2012:** the model's own. 538's published chances of winning are shown beside it as the check. A chance alone does not fix f(½), so no p is computed under 538 in those years.
- **2016:** 538's published forecast, polls-plus first, with polls-only and now-cast beside it.
  - Its projected share, made two-party, is the mean.
  - The sd its chance of winning implies, sd = (m − ½) / Φ⁻¹(P), with normal tails, is marked inferred.
  - The College stage uses the model's own national swing sd for that year, stated (capped at 0.95 of the smallest unit sd).
- **2020:** the Economist's own joint draws, with its published `p_decisive_vote` beside each state as the check. 538's forecast, with its own p, is beside it.
  - Where the Economist's draws are too few to fix p (35 safe states), the p shown is 538's.
  - Maine's and Nebraska's five districts use 538's, since the Economist does not model them.
- **1976 and 1980:** no p (below).

**538's forecasts** (rules of 04:12 and 04:20, the same for Congress):

- **The forecast used is the last dated before Election Day:** 2012-11-05, 2016-11-07 and 2020-11-02. 538's 2008 file holds Election Day only, so 2008's chances are dated Election Day, kept as the check and marked (04:20).
- **No source read says which 2016 model 538 showed by default.** The CSV and its repository's README are silent, so polls-plus is first and marked so.
- **The implied sd is kept only where it can be read.** It falls back to the model's own forecast, marked with the reason, where:
  - other candidates hold 1% or more of the chance (Utah 2016, 12.4% for McMullin and the rest);
  - the chance is exactly 0 or 1 (DC 2020);
  - the implied sd's estimated relative error is 20% or more.

  The error has two sources, combined in quadrature, and is recorded on every unit as `share.sd_rel_error`:
  - **The chance's sampling error.** 538 printed each chance as a count of simulation runs: every 2016 chance is a whole multiple of 0.00005 (20,000 runs) and every 2020 chance of 0.000025 (40,000 runs), measured; the run counts are inferred. The error is taken at the chance one standard error either side, not linearised. A chance of one run in 20,000 therefore comes out near 50% and falls back.
  - **The rounding of the printed shares**, to half their last digit.

  Florida 2016 comes out at 9.6%, North Carolina 2016 at 8.6% and ME-2 2016 at 10.5%, so all three keep 538's sd. Only Utah 2016 and DC 2020 fall back. In the College stage under 538, a unit that falls back takes the model's own sd.

### The model's own forecast (`pipeline/own_forecast.py`)

The orchestrator approved it at 03:51 under the maker's word of 02:38, in structure, with one change: the polls are the last national average before Election Day. It is built in the manner of Gelman, King and Boscardin (1998), as small as the data allow:

- **Uniform swing.** The state's last certified two-party share is moved by the national swing that 538's last national poll average before Election Day shows: v_it = v_i,t−4 + (N_t − V_t−4).
- **The errors.** The national error is normal with sd s_nat. Each state's own error is t with 4 degrees of freedom, matched on sd s_state.
- **The fit.** Both sds are fitted to earlier cycles only: the national error from 1976 on, the state errors from 1980 on. Each year's forecast uses only what was known before it.
- **The College stage** uses the same common swing, so it needs no further assumption.

### The Economist, 2020

Its 80,000 joint draws give both stages at once.

- For state i, take the draws whose share is within 0.01 of one half (a window of ±100 steps of 10⁻⁴, width 0.0201). K is the number of draws.
  - p_state = (draws in the window) ÷ (K × width) ÷ (2n);
  - W = (the draws in the window in which the state's electoral votes decide the College, weighted as above) ÷ (the draws in the window).
- **Where the weighted decisive count is under 10** (35 states), the draws cannot fix p. Instead:
  - the forecast carries `p_bound`, a 95% Poisson upper bound under its draws;
  - p falls to 538's, or to the model's own where 538's sd falls back too (DC).
- **A check on W for every state** reads the Economist's means, sds and correlation matrix as a multivariate normal (100,000 seeded draws given the tie). Its result is `W_mvn`.

### Maine and Nebraska

- Maine has split its electors since 1972 and Nebraska since 1992. Each district is its own unit with 1 electoral vote, and the statewide count decides 2.
- District contests are written from 2008. Their counts come from the Certificates of Ascertainment, read with pdftotext.
- Legible: Nebraska 2016, both states in 2020 and 2024. In Maine's 2020 certificate the at-large figure for the Republican ticket is illegible, so the FEC's statewide total is used, and noted.
- Unread, because the scans' text layer drops figures:
  - 2008, both states (no certificate read);
  - 2012, both states;
  - Maine 2016.

  These district contests have no result and no p, with the reason.
- **In the model's own forecast**, districts need the previous election's district counts, so they are modelled only in 2024. In the other years Maine and Nebraska are each one unit with all their votes, and the contest says so.
- 538 models the districts from 2012; the Economist treats both states whole.

### 1976 and 1980

These contests are written with results, populations and electoral votes, and p null with the reason. No published forecast was read for those years. The model's own forecast needs the state results of the two elections before, and 1968 and 1972 by state are in no standardized source read (the Clerk of the House's PDFs are the route; see Follow-ups).

- From its own draws, p = 1.13 × 10⁻⁷ (1 in 8.86 million), against its published `p_decisive_vote` of 1.14 × 10⁻⁷: a ratio of 0.988.

**Within each source:**

- **NARA:** each year's states sum to the printed total of electoral votes, and each candidate's column to its printed total.
- **The Census:** the state populations sum to the national figure, except a rounding difference of one person in 1984 (−1) and 1988 (+1), recorded.
- **538:** the candidates' chances sum to 1 in every unit. In the national row the remainder is the chance that no one wins a majority.
- **The Economist:** in every draw with no state printed at exactly one half, its published electoral-vote total equals the sum over the states the draw gives the Democrat (`draws_total_mismatch` 0 for every state). Where another state in a window draw is also printed at one half, the draw's total cannot say which of them went which way, and it is dropped (`dropped_unclear`, 19 draws over all 51 windows).
- **The certificates:** Nebraska's districts sum to its statewide figure; Maine's districts sum to no more than its at-large figure; and each statewide figure matches the FEC.
- **MEDSL:** the download's MD5 matches the Dataverse record.

## Notes on the data

- **1992, Maine and Utah.** The leading two candidates were not the two major-party nominees: Perot ran second in Maine and in Utah. The model's two-candidate share is of the nominees there, and the contest says so (`leading_two_not_nominees`).
- **Population.** Each contest gives the state's population and the nation's (`population_nation`). The law's per-person reading names the jurisdiction the office serves, which for the presidency is the nation; the brief names the state. The site can show either. District populations are not gathered yet.
- **n is ex post** in every contest, as the model allows, and is marked.
