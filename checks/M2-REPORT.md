## Summary

1. **Every p is what the model says it is**, from its contest's own recorded inputs, at every level. The presidency reproduces to 3 × 10⁻¹¹ (the model's own forecast) and exactly (538, the Economist). Congress reproduces within the bound set by the files' six-decimal rounding of mean and sd: 0 of 11,876 p blocks beyond it. No p is off by a factor of two; none is off at all beyond that rounding.
2. **The second stage holds** by methods unrelated to the pipeline's: the Electoral College's W to 3 × 10⁻¹² in every year 1984–2024 (Maine's and Nebraska's districts included), the chambers' P(decides | tie) to 10⁻¹² (Senate) and 3 × 10⁻⁷ (House), and Monte Carlo within 3 standard errors everywhere.
3. **The forecasts are honest**, with four things to weigh: (a) D2's diagnosis of the governors ("too wide in the middle, so p too high") is not supported; the forecast is slightly too wide everywhere, which makes p slightly too *low*, and the 0-of-4.4 is specific to the 5,000-vote line. (b) Leave-one-cycle-out versus a fit on earlier cycles only moves the sum of p by up to 19% in the early cycles (House 2014) and not at all in 2024. (c) The presidency's 2024 horizon is matched to within 3 days, as closely as the sources allow. (d) 538's 20% rule guards the sd's error, not p's; p's error is |z² − 1| times the sd's, and 174 of the 1,320 538-first entries exceed 20% on p.
4. **The published gaps are explained** as far as the sources allow: the Economist's far-state gaps come from a different estimator of the same draws (a fitted tail where the window holds a dozen draws) and its level from a different n, while a ± 20–40% scatter in WI, NH, MN, NV and AZ remains that neither estimator reproduces from these draws; GKB's 1992 figure rests on an earlier, wider forecast, not a different method; GSE's 2008 figures rest on poll-based state means and narrower state errors.
5. **The presidential record matches the forecasts** at every margin from 1 vote to 100,000.
6. **Every first-screen figure reproduces by hand.**

## 2. Reasoning

### 2.1 Item 1: every p from its own inputs

**Method.** `c1_recompute.py` reads each contest's `p` block, finds the forecast its `under` names among the contest's own `forecasts`, and recomputes f(½) and p = f(½)/(2n) with `m2lib`'s own normal and t4 densities (t4 at scale sd/√2) and, for the model's own presidential forecast, its own convolution of the normal national error with the t4 state error by quadrature (`own_pres_pdf`). The Economist's p is recounted from the raw draws (`c2`). n is the contest's `n_other_voters`.

**Tolerances, and why.** Presidency, the model's own: 10⁻⁴, because D1 integrates on a 401-point grid and I by adaptive quadrature; the two should agree far better than that, and do (2.6 × 10⁻¹¹). Closed forms (538, the Economist's counts, the event, the product): 10⁻⁹ or exact. Congress: the files record mean and sd to six decimals, while p was computed from the unrounded values, so the recorded mean and sd cannot give the recorded p exactly; the bound for each contest is the largest relative change in f(½) over the four corners mean ± 5 × 10⁻⁷, sd ± 5 × 10⁻⁷, plus 10⁻⁹. Every difference lies inside its bound (the largest at 0.999 of it), so the differences are the rounding and nothing else is visible beneath it. The loop is closed from the other side by `c4`, which rebuilds the model's own forecast from the contests' recorded inputs (lean, incumbency, midterm, president's party) and reproduces every governor mean and sd to 5.3 × 10⁻⁷, the rounding again [measured, L615].

*Two readings for the orchestrator.* The record's p is right; a reader recomputing p from the file's own mean and sd will differ from it by up to 7 × 10⁻⁴ relative (typically 10⁻⁵). Either leave it (p is the figure; mean and sd are shown rounded) or record mean and sd, or f(½), to more digits so that the file reproduces itself. I choose neither.

**The exact sum.** No contest is near the switch (1.7). For every Congress p the lattice sum of MATH §1.2(a), ½ P(X = n/2) with X | v ~ Binomial(n, v) integrated against the forecast over (0, 1), is within 0.4% of the density form where p ≥ 10⁻⁶ and within 1% where p ≥ 10⁻⁸. Beyond that the density form runs low by up to 6% at z = 8.8, where p is 10⁻²¹. The model's convolved form (MATH §1.3, variance sd² + 1/(4n)) reproduces the lattice sum to 0.11% everywhere, so the error is understood; what MATH §1.3 does not say is that its "2% once n sd² > 6.25" holds at mean ½ and the error grows with z. A line in MATH would close it; no p on the site is affected. (A forecast truncated to (0, 1) and renormalized, which the model does not do, would raise the far seats' p by 1/mass: 1.31 for 2018-house-NY-15 at mean 0.96; 1.000 for every p ≥ 10⁻⁶ except by 10⁻⁴.)

### 2.2 Item 2: the second stage

**The College.** D1's W is a 401-point grid over the national swing with the other units' electoral votes counted by convolution of their win chances. `c2_college.py` counts them instead by a Fourier (characteristic-function) product on the unit circle with prefix and suffix products, so that each unit's "others" distribution comes from one pass, on a 1,201-point Simpson grid over ± 12 s_d; and, unrelated to both, by importance-sampling Monte Carlo (10⁶ draws of the national swing and the state errors with the unit held at the tie, the window weight averaged). Fourier agrees with the record to 3 × 10⁻¹², which is what two convergent quadratures of a smooth integrand should give; Monte Carlo agrees within its standard error for all 573 unit-years (largest |z| 2.47; about 0.1 beyond 3 se expected by chance, 0 seen). The districts of 2024 (ME-1, ME-2, NE-1, NE-2, NE-3) are in the count with their own electoral vote and sd. Under 538's marginals the same, with the national swing's sd from the model's own year, which is what the record's method text says and what the code does [measured]. The Economist's 204 window counts are recounted exactly from the raw file.

One finding (2.4): where the Economist's window holds a dozen weighted draws (OH 13, IA 12, CO 28), W depends on the window's width by factors of 3 to 10. The record marks a W as thin only under 10 weighted draws, so OH and IA are not marked. That is a labelling question, not an error: the record says what it counted.

**The chambers.** `c3_chambers.py` rebuilds each chamber-year's inputs from the files (every seat's mean, whether modelled, inferred or fixed; the seats not up; the pivots), and counts the other seats by the same Fourier product on a Simpson grid, with the swing given the tie as MATH §4.2 writes it, d | tie ~ N((½ − μ) s_a²/sd², s_a² s_e²/sd²). The pivots are read from the record (House 217 of the others; Senate 49 or 50 less the Democrats not up and the fixed seats, with the Vice President at ½ in presidential years, as the record now stands). Every Senate seat agrees to 10⁻¹², every House seat to 3 × 10⁻⁷ (the House's sums run over 400 seats and 10⁻⁶ was the tolerance set); the unconditional P(decides), recorded for comparison, to 4 × 10⁻¹⁴. Monte Carlo (2 × 10⁶ draws per Senate year, 4 × 10⁵ for the House of 2018, 2022 and 2024) agrees within its error for every seat. *Not checked:* the `P_decides_given_tie_if_VP_D` and `_if_VP_R` fields; when D2's Vice-President change lands, the presidential-year Senate seats' P(decides | tie) change and `c3` should be rerun on them.

**538 × own (2.6).** Under 538 the control p is 538's seat p times P(decides | tie), and P conditions the national swing on a tie at the **model's own** mean for the seat, with the other seats the model's own. Arithmetically this is exactly what the record says (1.10). As a model it holds two forecasts of the same seat at once: the chance of a tie is 538's, but what a tie *tells* the chamber is the model's. Where the two means agree it is harmless; where 538 puts a seat at 0.48 and the model at 0.33 (2018-house-WV-03), a tie is a small surprise to 538 and a large swing to the model, and P is 0.006 as recorded against 0.023 conditioned on 538's mean. Over all seats the medians are near one (table 2.6), the extremes are factors, and the sum of control p moves by −30% (House 2020, reading c) to +11% (House 2018, reading b). *Two readings.* (a) Keep it as recorded and say so in the method text (the record does): a published seat forecast through the model's chamber stage. (b) or (c) Condition on 538's mean, or take 538's mean and sd as the seat's marginal with s_e,i = √(sd₅₃₈² − s_a²), as the presidency already treats 538's marginals; this makes the seat and the chamber see one world, at the price of mixing 538's one-seat sd with the model's correlation. Reading (c) is the presidency's rule; it is the one that moves most.

### 2.3 Item 3: the forecasts

**The governors.** D2's arithmetic holds: under the record's p (the site's mix, 152 own and 66 538-first), 4.38 governor races were expected within 5,000 votes and none came, P 0.013. The explanation offered, "the forecast runs too wide in the middle so p is too high", fails on both counts. First, the forecast is not too wide in the middle in share: counting races by |share − ½| ≤ h gives expected/seen of 3.7/3, 7.5/6, 14.9/17, 29.7/34, 72.6/82 for h from a quarter point to five points, so the record has, if anything, slightly *more* near-even races than forecast at every width above half a point. Second, a forecast that is too wide has too little density at its centre, so a too-wide forecast gives p too *low*; narrowing it (A3) raises p by 4%. The forecast is about 5% too wide overall (z sd 0.95), not in the middle particularly (the band ratios are 0.96, 0.94, 0.95 for |mean − ½| up to 10 points and 0.86 beyond it). ### 2.4 Item 4: the published figures

**The Economist, 2020.** The Economist does not state its method for `p_decisive_vote` [stated, D1], and its public repository (157 files at commit b89e602) holds no code for it [measured]. Two things explain the spread. (i) *Which estimator.* The record counts ties in a ± 0.01 window of the share and weights by whether the unit decides: an empirical count. GSE's method (model §4; MATH §3.4) multiplies P(the state is necessary) by the density at zero of a t4 fitted to the vote margin among the necessary draws. `c5_published.py` runs GSE's method on the Economist's own draws: it gives 0.903 of the published figure in the median over all 51 states (0.35–1.60; within 25% in 30), the same ratio the record's window has over its 16, and it runs at 0.86–0.98 of the window's own count where the window holds hundreds of draws (PA 0.98, MI 0.86, FL 0.91, AZ 0.89, GA 0.89, NC 0.89, TX 0.90), the t4 fit's shape being a little flatter at zero than the draws. So Ohio's 0.14 is the gap between an empirical count and a fitted tail in a state 3.4 sd from necessity, where the draws cannot say which is right. ## 7. Reasoning of the second pass

### 7.1 Items 1 and 2: Congress after #38 and #51, and D2's tests

`c8_recompute2.py` reads every contest of `data/congress/` and `data/governor/` and recomputes each p block's chain from the file's own numbers with `m2lib`: f(½) from the mean and sd (normal, or t4 at sd/√2), the near-tie multiplier F/(1 + (F − 1)q), p = f(½)/(2n) × multiplier, the event, the control p; and each year's chamber block from its fit (c·z, k s_a √(1 + z(Z′Z)⁻¹z′), the shrunk mean). Everything reproduces to machine precision (7.1), which is what it should be: these are closed forms on recorded inputs. `c9_fit2.py` and `m2fit.py` rebuild D2's rows from Cha, MEDSL, The Downballot and the files, with the House specials on the lines of t − 2 as the files have them since #51, and refit every year: the coefficients agree to 10⁻⁷, the k and λ to 10⁻⁷, the near-tie factors to 10⁻⁵, and every contest's mean to 10⁻⁶ (7.2). `c12_control2.py` repeats the first pass's Fourier count with the Vice-President's branches: every year to 10⁻¹³ (7.3). The 2024 π is now the College chance with the districts (0.5295), the first pass's reading 3.5(5) settled by D2's #51. `c13_governors2.py` repeats 3.1 after the last-race term: the share calibration holds and the 0 within 5,000 votes stands (P 0.009 on 2006–2024; over 1986–2024 5 against 10.4, P 0.052). The reading of the first pass stands: the count at 5,000 is the unlucky line, not the forecast's middle.

`c10_backtest2.py` reproduces D2's four tests from my own refit (8.1) and then asks whether the record's adoptions follow the rule as REPORT §0.1, 2.6 and 2.7 write it (8.2). They do, once the fourth condition D2 added in #51 is counted: the governors' t4 passes the three tests on the full years and fails the leave-one-year-out test without 2010, 2018 or 2020, and D2 adopted none. 8.3 is the reading that matters for the orchestrator: the House's shrinkage decision sits on 0.001 of an expected count in its tightest safe cell and fails the robustness test, so its rejection is right by the rule and arbitrary by the margin; and the Senate's near-tie level scheme, adopted as of #51's record, fails the same robustness test without 2020 (its band without 2024). The record then applied the robustness test to the variants, not to the near-tie schemes; D2's #53 (09:10) applied it to both, with round 5's cells added to the schemes' test, and the Senate now carries no near-tie factor, the House its level scheme. `m2fit.near_tie_test` carries that rule since this pass merged #53 and reproduces every verdict of `backtest.json`'s `round_5_cells`, `leave_one_year_out` and `robust` (324 numbers, 0 beyond tolerance): the Senate's level scheme fails the 48–52% cell of the 10–20 point band on the full years (none 5.52, level 5.80, 5 seen) and without 2012, 2014 and 2024, and the safe seats' 49–51% count without 2020. I note that the near-tie test's cells for the Senate hold 5 to 18 seen, so one year's absence changes the count by a quarter.

### 7.2 Item 3: k

`c11_k_reading.py` scores, on earlier years only and in the way D2's variants were scored, four alternatives to the record's k (which is fitted on all earlier years): k from the last one, two or three years' residuals, and a trend. The record's forecasts are too wide out of sample (9.1); the last-year fits tighten them to z rms 0.93–1.00 and raise the House's and Senate's log score (9.2). But every one of them moves the 49–51% count and the safe-band counts further from what was seen, and the mechanism is plain: in a seat 10 points from even a narrower sd lowers the density at ½, and the record's near-tie counts already run below what was seen there (House 0.10–0.20 band: 14.2 expected against 16 seen; Senate 3.0 against 4). The near-tie factor is the record's correction for exactly that, and a tighter k would have to be fitted together with it. The orchestrator's word (09:03) is that D2 runs only its untested half-life variant, then the incumbency variants, under the robustness test; the scores here are on the same tests so they can be read beside those.

### 7.3 Item 4: D3's levels

**Every p from its inputs.** `c14_d3_recompute.py` reads each contest of the four levels through `m2d3.slim` and checks every recorded number against my own: the pair's n and share, the window and its widening (k ≥ 10), N ≥ 100, q to five decimals, f(½) = q/(2h), p = f(½)/(2n), the event, the method rule (exact where n < 1,000 or n sd_eq² < 6.25), for the partisan route the normal and t4 densities within an envelope of the file's 4-decimal mean and sd, and since #59 the mass on every like-race exact block and the factor, key, fitting years and value of every `calibration_not_of_record` block (10.2). All pass. The run of 09:40 had one finding (10.3): the pipeline's lattice sum integrated a normal cut to (0, 1) and renormalised, and MATH §1.2 did not say so. For Congress this is invisible (the first pass noted it as a possible reading, × 1.31 for the farthest seat). For a like-race class it was not: the class's f is a measured density, sd_eq is the normal that has that density at ½, and a class with f = 1.2 has sd_eq 0.33, so a third of the normal's mass lies outside (0, 1) and the renormalised sum was 1.5 times the plain one; 503 like-race exact blocks were affected beyond the rounding, every one by exactly 1/mass. The orchestrator's reading (09:03) assigned the fix to D3's route and kept `common.py`'s cut for forecasts of a share (MATH §1's note, #52); D3's #59 multiplies the renormalised sum by the normal's mass on the sum's grid and records the mass, and every exact block of the like-race routes now equals the plain sum of MATH §1.2 at sd_eq, the mass with it.

**The pools.** `c15_d3_refit.py` rebuilds every like-race pool from the files alone (the state levels from Klarner's contests, the local pools from MEDSL's and the State Board's) and from Klarner's contest file (route (a)). The classes and counts reproduce exactly in every pool (10.4, 10.5, 10.6). Three conventions had to be read from the pipeline to get there, and all three are worth a line in D3's REPORT (the third is there since #59): a file's "built by" names the build that wrote the year's file, so a State Board contest of May 2018 sits in a file `local_medsl.py` wrote while its forecast is North Carolina's route's (the route is the source and the date: MEDSL's, or the State Board's November contests in MEDSL's place); a year whose state fails its check (California's and Wyoming's 2022, Indiana's, Louisiana's and New Jersey's 2024) is left out of every pool that year, which the files say only contest by contest in the reason; and the State Board's route reads a November contest whose name was on a ballot earlier that year (a nonpartisan primary) as a contest after an earlier round, leaves it out of its pool and computes no p for it on its own dates, while MEDSL's route writes the same contest as a general with p. The run of 09:40 had read those 35 contests as generals in the State Board's pool too, which is why that pool ran 1 to 17 rows long in 656 contests; read as the route reads them, all 1,750 agree. Whether the State Board's route should read them as generals, as MEDSL's does, is a choice D3's REPORT §9 leaves to the orchestrator; it moves which contests a route takes, not any p of record.

**Route (a).** The fit on all years and the back-test by earlier years reproduce REPORT §2 and §3 to the printed digits (10.7). Per contest, my fit on earlier years only gives the record's mean within 5 × 10⁻⁴ in 97.6% of 72,466 contests, which is the size of difference a fit on rows that differ by a few of ninety thousand makes; the 13 beyond 5 × 10⁻³ are all in chamber-years whose lean differs. At 09:34 I read the lean difference as the pipeline selecting the rows by who finished first and second; that reading was wrong (10.8), and D3's #59 shows it: the helper lists every named candidate but the write-ins, and `partisan_rows` keeps the single-seat generals with exactly one Democrat and one Republican on that list, which is the ballot's rule. The rows differ because mine come from Klarner's contest-level flags and the pipeline's from its candidate rows, and the two disagree in 30 contests [stated, D3]; by chamber-year, my rows and the files' candidate rows under the ballot rule differ in 24 chamber-years by 96 rows of 91,018. Nothing for the orchestrator here.

**The #31 factor** (10.9). Since #59 neither local factor is of record: D3 applies the orchestrator's robustness test (08:35) as a rule in `local_medsl.py` (every scored year with its own factor, fitted on the earlier years; adopted only if the whole improves and no tested cell, with 5 or more seen or expected, is made worse, and still so with any one scored year left out), and the like-race factor fails it on the cells (local judge, other local office, sheriff or constable, in the full test and without 2022), while the partisan factor fails the council cell and has no second year. My implementation of that rule gives the record's every number: the factors, the fitting counts by office type, the contests, seen, expected and deviance before and after, the cells, and the verdicts. The p each factor would have given is kept in `calibration_not_of_record` (3,336 blocks), which 10.2 checks. The arithmetic behind the verdict is as at 09:40: the like-race factor rests on 2020 (1,812 contests, 249 seen against 317 expected), the only year with the weight to fit one, and 2022 (110 contests, North Carolina's November) cannot stand in for it.

**The boundary route** (10.10). Route (c) had not been checked against outcomes. It is calibrated as a whole and by class, and the record's `backtest.json` cells reproduce from my pools. Two things the whole hides: by the deciding pair's size the forecast runs 12% low under 3,000 votes and 20% high over 30,000, the same shape route (a) answers with s_e by size bin, and the class density could too (the orchestrator chooses); and the exact margins (0 or 1 vote) run above the smooth forecast in the multi-seat classes (4+ seats: 7 seen against 2.9), which I read as slates: candidates of one party in a multi-seat race often carry identical counts, so the k-th and (k + 1)-th tie more often than a density predicts [inferred].
