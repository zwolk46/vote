3. **The forecasts are honest**, with four things to weigh: (a) D2's diagnosis of the governors ("too wide in the middle, so p too high") is not supported; the forecast is slightly too wide everywhere, which makes p slightly too *low*, and the 0-of-4.4 is specific to the 5,000-vote line. (c) The presidency's 2024 horizon is matched to within 3 days, as closely as the sources allow. (d) 538's 20% rule guards the sd's error, not p's; p's error is |z² − 1| times the sd's, and 174 of the 1,320 538-first entries exceed 20% on p.
4. **The published gaps are explained** as far as the sources allow: the Economist's far-state gaps come from a different estimator of the same draws (a fitted tail where the window holds a dozen draws) and its level from a different n, while a ± 20–40% scatter in WI, NH, MN, NV and AZ remains that neither estimator reproduces from these draws; GKB's 1992 figure rests on an earlier, wider forecast, not a different method; GSE's 2008 figures rest on poll-based state means and narrower state errors.
5. **The presidential record matches the forecasts** at every margin from 1 vote to 100,000.
6. **Every first-screen figure reproduces by hand.**

## 2. Reasoning

### 2.1 Item 1: every p from its own inputs

**Method.** `c1_recompute.py` reads each contest's `p` block, finds the forecast its `under` names among the contest's own `forecasts`, and recomputes f(½) and p = f(½)/(2n) with `m2lib`'s own normal and t4 densities (t4 at scale sd/√2) and, for the model's own presidential forecast, its own convolution of the normal national error with the t4 state error by quadrature (`own_pres_pdf`). The Economist's p is recounted from the raw draws (`c2`). n is the contest's `n_other_voters`.

**Tolerances, and why.** Presidency, the model's own: 10⁻⁴, because D1 integrates on a 401-point grid and I by adaptive quadrature; the two should agree far better than that, and do (2.6 × 10⁻¹¹). Closed forms (538, the Economist's counts, the event, the product): 10⁻⁹ or exact. Congress: the files record mean and sd to six decimals, while p was computed from the unrounded values, so the recorded mean and sd cannot give the recorded p exactly; the bound for each contest is the largest relative change in f(½) over the four corners mean ± 5 × 10⁻⁷, sd ± 5 × 10⁻⁷, plus 10⁻⁹. Every difference lies inside its bound (the largest at 0.999 of it), so the differences are the rounding and nothing else is visible beneath it. The loop is closed from the other side by `c4`, which rebuilds the model's own forecast from the contests' recorded inputs (lean, incumbency, midterm, president's party) and reproduces every governor mean and sd to 5.3 × 10⁻⁷, the rounding again [measured, L615].

*Two readings for the orchestrator.* The record's p is right; a reader recomputing p from the file's own mean and sd will differ from it by up to 7 × 10⁻⁴ relative (typically 10⁻⁵). Either leave it (p is the figure; mean and sd are shown rounded) or record mean and sd, or f(½), to more digits so that the file reproduces itself. I choose neither.

**The exact sum.** No contest is near the switch (1.7). For every Congress p the lattice sum of MATH §1.2(a), ½ P(X = n/2) with X | v ~ Binomial(n, v) integrated against the forecast over (0, 1), is within 0.4% of the density form where p ≥ 10⁻⁶ and within 1% where p ≥ 10⁻⁸. Beyond that the density form runs low by up to 6% at z = 8.8, where p is 10⁻²¹. The model's convolved form (MATH §1.3, variance sd² + 1/(4n)) reproduces the lattice sum to 0.11% everywhere, so the error is understood; what MATH §1.3 does not say is that its "2% once n sd² > 6.25" holds at mean ½ and the error grows with z. A line in MATH would close it; no p on the site is affected. (A forecast truncated to (0, 1) and renormalized, which the model does not do, would raise the far seats' p by 1/mass: 1.31 for 2018-house-NY-15 at mean 0.96; 1.000 for every p ≥ 10⁻⁶ except by 10⁻⁴.)

### 2.2 Item 2: the second stage

**The College.** D1's W is a 401-point grid over the national swing with the other units' electoral votes counted by convolution of their win chances. `c2_college.py` counts them instead by a Fourier (characteristic-function) product on the unit circle with prefix and suffix products, so that each unit's "others" distribution comes from one pass, on a 1,201-point Simpson grid over ± 12 s_d; and, unrelated to both, by importance-sampling Monte Carlo (10⁶ draws of the national swing and the state errors with the unit held at the tie, the window weight averaged). Fourier agrees with the record to 3 × 10⁻¹², which is what two convergent quadratures of a smooth integrand should give; Monte Carlo agrees within its standard error for all 573 unit-years (largest |z| 2.47; about 0.1 beyond 3 se expected by chance, 0 seen). The districts of 2024 (ME-1, ME-2, NE-1, NE-2, NE-3) are in the count with their own electoral vote and sd. Under 538's marginals the same, with the national swing's sd from the model's own year, which is what the record's method text says and what the code does [measured]. The Economist's 204 window counts are recounted exactly from the raw file.

One finding (2.4): where the Economist's window holds a dozen weighted draws (OH 13, IA 12, CO 28), W depends on the window's width by factors of 3 to 10. The record marks a W as thin only under 10 weighted draws, so OH and IA are not marked. That is a labelling question, not an error: the record says what it counted.

**The chambers.** `c3_chambers.py` rebuilds each chamber-year's inputs from the files (every seat's mean, whether modelled, inferred or fixed; the seats not up; the pivots), and counts the other seats by the same Fourier product on a Simpson grid, with the swing given the tie as MATH §4.2 writes it, d | tie ~ N((½ − μ) s_a²/sd², s_a² s_e²/sd²). The pivots are read from the record (House 217 of the others; Senate 49 or 50 less the Democrats not up and the fixed seats, with the Vice President at ½ in presidential years, as the record now stands). Every Senate seat agrees to 10⁻¹², every House seat to 3 × 10⁻⁷ (the House's sums run over 400 seats and 10⁻⁶ was the tolerance set); the unconditional P(decides), recorded for comparison, to 4 × 10⁻¹⁴. Monte Carlo (2 × 10⁶ draws per Senate year, 4 × 10⁵ for the House of 2018, 2022 and 2024) agrees within its error for every seat. *Not checked:* the `P_decides_given_tie_if_VP_D` and `_if_VP_R` fields; when D2's Vice-President change lands, the presidential-year Senate seats' P(decides | tie) change and `c3` should be rerun on them.

**538 × own (2.6).** Under 538 the control p is 538's seat p times P(decides | tie), and P conditions the national swing on a tie at the **model's own** mean for the seat, with the other seats the model's own. Arithmetically this is exactly what the record says (1.10). As a model it holds two forecasts of the same seat at once: the chance of a tie is 538's, but what a tie *tells* the chamber is the model's. Where the two means agree it is harmless; where 538 puts a seat at 0.48 and the model at 0.33 (2018-house-WV-03), a tie is a small surprise to 538 and a large swing to the model, and P is 0.006 as recorded against 0.023 conditioned on 538's mean. Over all seats the medians are near one (table 2.6), the extremes are factors, and the sum of control p moves by −30% (House 2020, reading c) to +11% (House 2018, reading b). *Two readings.* (a) Keep it as recorded and say so in the method text (the record does): a published seat forecast through the model's chamber stage. (b) or (c) Condition on 538's mean, or take 538's mean and sd as the seat's marginal with s_e,i = √(sd₅₃₈² − s_a²), as the presidency already treats 538's marginals; this makes the seat and the chamber see one world, at the price of mixing 538's one-seat sd with the model's correlation. Reading (c) is the presidency's rule; it is the one that moves most.

### 2.3 Item 3: the forecasts

**The governors.** D2's arithmetic holds: under the record's p (the site's mix, 152 own and 66 538-first), 4.38 governor races were expected within 5,000 votes and none came, P 0.013. The explanation offered, "the forecast runs too wide in the middle so p is too high", fails on both counts. First, the forecast is not too wide in the middle in share: counting races by |share − ½| ≤ h gives expected/seen of 3.7/3, 7.5/6, 14.9/17, 29.7/34, 72.6/82 for h from a quarter point to five points, so the record has, if anything, slightly *more* near-even races than forecast at every width above half a point. Second, a forecast that is too wide has too little density at its centre, so a too-wide forecast gives p too *low*; narrowing it (A3) raises p by 4%. The forecast is about 5% too wide overall (z sd 0.95), not in the middle particularly (the band ratios are 0.96, 0.94, 0.95 for |mean − ½| up to 10 points and 0.86 beyond it). *Options, with what each does to p (none applied, each leave-one-cycle-out like the record):* A1 t4 tails at the same sd: p × 0.997 in the median but 0.63 to 1.32 across races, and the 50% interval worse (0.54), so a worse forecast; A2 an error sd that varies with |mean − ½|: z sd 1.000 and p × 1.046 (0.96–1.12), the cleanest calibration; A3 the sd scaled by the back-test's 0.93: z sd 1.000, p × 1.039 (0.86–1.08), the simplest; A4 a lagged term, the state's last governor race, which D2's method lacks: on the 187 races of 2010–2024 that have one, b_lag = +0.25, s_e 0.0666 against 0.0702 without it on the same rows, log score 1.064 → 1.143, p × 1.030 (0.60–1.32), the most informative and the one that changes the method rather than its scale. ### 2.4 Item 4: the published figures

**The Economist, 2020.** The Economist does not state its method for `p_decisive_vote` [stated, D1], and its public repository (157 files at commit b89e602) holds no code for it [measured]. Two things explain the spread. (i) *Which estimator.* The record counts ties in a ± 0.01 window of the share and weights by whether the unit decides: an empirical count. GSE's method (model §4; MATH §3.4) multiplies P(the state is necessary) by the density at zero of a t4 fitted to the vote margin among the necessary draws. `c5_published.py` runs GSE's method on the Economist's own draws: it gives 0.903 of the published figure in the median over all 51 states (0.35–1.60; within 25% in 30), the same ratio the record's window has over its 16, and it runs at 0.86–0.98 of the window's own count where the window holds hundreds of draws (PA 0.98, MI 0.86, FL 0.91, AZ 0.89, GA 0.89, NC 0.89, TX 0.90), the t4 fit's shape being a little flatter at zero than the draws. Where the window holds a dozen draws the two part: for Ohio the window counts 13 weighted draws (6.9 × 10⁻¹⁰), the density among the necessary draws is 6.7 × 10⁻¹⁰ by a local count, and a t4 *fitted* to those draws puts 1.76 × 10⁻⁹ at zero because the fit's tail is heavier than the draws' own; the published figure is 4.98 × 10⁻⁹. So Ohio's 0.14 is the gap between an empirical count and a fitted tail in a state 3.4 sd from necessity, where the draws cannot say which is right. (ii) *Which n.* At the level, both the window and GSE-t4 sit at about 0.90 of the published figure; if the Economist's method is GSE's, the n it implies is 0.82 of the 2020 count and 0.97 of the 2016 count in the median, which is consistent with the Economist having used 2016-sized turnout [inferred; the Economist does not say]. (iii) *What remains.* In Wisconsin, New Hampshire, Minnesota and Nevada both estimators sit at 0.62–0.68 (GSE-t4) and 0.67–0.92 (the window) of the published figure, and in Arizona at 1.18 and 1.32; these windows hold 135 to 600 weighted draws, so sampling error (4–9%) does not cover it, and no reading of these draws I have tried (window widths, a local-linear fit, GSE with normal or t4 tails at either scale) reproduces them. The published figure is then not a function of these 80,000 draws by either method, which points to another draw set or another n per state (turnout by state is the one input neither method here varies). *Likelier right:* for the site's definition — the chance of a tie by the draws' own count, with the certified n — the record's figure, whose counts are exact. The Economist's is a different estimator with an unstated n and, it seems, other draws; it is not wrong, it is a different question. *What would settle it:* the Economist's script for `p_decisive_vote`, or its per-state n and draw set.

## 7. Reasoning of the second pass

### 7.1 Items 1 and 2: Congress after #38 and #51, and D2's tests

`c8_recompute2.py` reads every contest of `data/congress/` and `data/governor/` and recomputes each p block's chain from the file's own numbers with `m2lib`: f(½) from the mean and sd (normal, or t4 at sd/√2), the near-tie multiplier F/(1 + (F − 1)q), p = f(½)/(2n) × multiplier, the event, the control p; and each year's chamber block from its fit (c·z, k s_a √(1 + z(Z′Z)⁻¹z′), the shrunk mean). Everything reproduces to machine precision (7.1), which is what it should be: these are closed forms on recorded inputs. `c9_fit2.py` and `m2fit.py` rebuild D2's rows from Cha, MEDSL, The Downballot and the files, with the House specials on the lines of t − 2 as the files have them since #51, and refit every year: the coefficients agree to 10⁻⁷, the k and λ to 10⁻⁷, the near-tie factors to 10⁻⁵, and every contest's mean to 10⁻⁶ (7.2). `c12_control2.py` repeats the first pass's Fourier count with the Vice-President's branches: every year to 10⁻¹³ (7.3). They do, once the fourth condition D2 added in #51 is counted: the governors' t4 passes the three tests on the full years and fails the leave-one-year-out test without 2010, 2018 or 2020, and D2 adopted none. 8.3 is the reading that matters for the orchestrator: the House's shrinkage decision sits on 0.001 of an expected count in its tightest safe cell and fails the robustness test, so its rejection is right by the rule and arbitrary by the margin; and the Senate's near-tie level scheme, adopted as of #51's record, fails the same robustness test without 2020 (its band without 2024). ### 7.2 Item 3: k

`c11_k_reading.py` scores, on earlier years only and in the way D2's variants were scored, four alternatives to the record's k (which is fitted on all earlier years): k from the last one, two or three years' residuals, and a trend.
