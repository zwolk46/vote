# M2: every computed p, checked against the model

*Written 2026-11-07 by M2 (brief `briefs/M2-check-computed-p.md`), on branch `claude/project-thread-pbr8zl`. What was checked: every p in `data/president/` and `data/congress/` as they stand on `main` (D1's #23 and D2's #24; unchanged by the later merges). The standard is `model/MODEL.md` and `model/MATH.md`. The levels D3 brings (`data/state-upper/`, `data/state-lower/`, #25) are a second pass on the orchestrator's word and are not checked here. Nothing outside `checks/` is changed; no data and no pipeline.*

**Reproduce.** `sh checks/m2/run_all.sh` from the repository root writes `checks/m2/OUTPUT.txt` (about 35 minutes). Every number in this report is on the OUTPUT.txt line it cites (**L** = line of OUTPUT.txt). The scripts in `checks/m2/` import nothing from `pipeline/` or `model/`; `m2lib.py` is their own implementation of the model's formulas. The sources they read are fetched outside the repository (`m2lib.CACHE`: the Economist's `economist_model_output.zip`, 538's four checking-our-work CSVs, the Economist's `us-potus-model` README) and their sha256 is printed and matched to the record on each run.

**Marks.** [measured] a number this run computes from the record and the sources; [stated] a source's or a report's own words; [inferred] my reading of measured things.

## Summary

1. **Every p is what the model says it is**, from its contest's own recorded inputs, at every level. The presidency reproduces to 3 × 10⁻¹¹ (the model's own forecast) and exactly (538, the Economist). Congress reproduces within the bound set by the files' six-decimal rounding of mean and sd: 0 of 11,876 p blocks beyond it. No p is off by a factor of two; none is off at all beyond that rounding.
2. **The second stage holds** by methods unrelated to the pipeline's: the Electoral College's W to 3 × 10⁻¹² in every year 1984–2024 (Maine's and Nebraska's districts included), the chambers' P(decides | tie) to 10⁻¹² (Senate) and 3 × 10⁻⁷ (House), and Monte Carlo within 3 standard errors everywhere.
3. **The forecasts are honest**, with four things to weigh: (a) D2's diagnosis of the governors ("too wide in the middle, so p too high") is not supported; the forecast is slightly too wide everywhere, which makes p slightly too *low*, and the 0-of-4.4 is specific to the 5,000-vote line. (b) Leave-one-cycle-out versus a fit on earlier cycles only moves the sum of p by up to 19% in the early cycles (House 2014) and not at all in 2024. (c) The presidency's 2024 horizon is matched to within 3 days, as closely as the sources allow. (d) 538's 20% rule guards the sd's error, not p's; p's error is |z² − 1| times the sd's, and 174 of the 1,320 538-first entries exceed 20% on p.
4. **The published gaps are explained** as far as the sources allow: the Economist's far-state gaps come from a different estimator of the same draws (a fitted tail where the window holds a dozen draws) and its level from a different n, while a ± 20–40% scatter in WI, NH, MN, NV and AZ remains that neither estimator reproduces from these draws; GKB's 1992 figure rests on an earlier, wider forecast, not a different method; GSE's 2008 figures rest on poll-based state means and narrower state errors.
5. **The presidential record matches the forecasts** at every margin from 1 vote to 100,000.
6. **Every first-screen figure reproduces by hand.**

## 2. Reasoning

### 2.1 Item 1: every p from its own inputs

**Method.** `c1_recompute.py` reads each contest's `p` block, finds the forecast its `under` names among the contest's own `forecasts`, and recomputes f(½) and p = f(½)/(2n) with `m2lib`'s own normal and t4 densities (t4 at scale sd/√2) and, for the model's own presidential forecast, its own convolution of the normal national error with the t4 state error by quadrature (`own_pres_pdf`). The Economist's p is recounted from the raw draws (`c2`). n is the contest's `n_other_voters`.

**Tolerances, and why.** Presidency, the model's own: 10⁻⁴, because D1 integrates on a 401-point grid and I by adaptive quadrature; the two should agree far better than that, and do (2.6 × 10⁻¹¹). Closed forms (538, the Economist's counts, the event, the product): 10⁻⁹ or exact. Congress: the files record mean and sd to six decimals, while p was computed from the unrounded values, so the recorded mean and sd cannot give the recorded p exactly; the bound for each contest is the largest relative change in f(½) over the four corners mean ± 5 × 10⁻⁷, sd ± 5 × 10⁻⁷, plus 10⁻⁹. Every difference lies inside its bound (the largest at 0.999 of it), so the differences are the rounding and nothing else is visible beneath it. The loop is closed from the other side by `c4`, which rebuilds the model's own forecast from the contests' recorded inputs (lean, incumbency, midterm, president's party) and reproduces every governor mean and sd to 5.3 × 10⁻⁷, the rounding again [measured, L615].

*Two readings for the orchestrator.* The record's p is right; a reader recomputing p from the file's own mean and sd will differ from it by up to 7 × 10⁻⁴ relative (typically 10⁻⁵). Either leave it (p is the figure; mean and sd are shown rounded) or record mean and sd, or f(½), to more digits so that the file reproduces itself. I choose neither.

**The exact sum.** No contest is near the switch (1.7). For every Congress p the lattice sum of MATH §1.2(a), ½ P(X = n/2) with X | v ~ Binomial(n, v) integrated against the forecast over (0, 1), is within 0.4% of the density form where p ≥ 10⁻⁶ and within 1% where p ≥ 10⁻⁸. Beyond that the density form runs low by up to 6% at z = 8.8, where p is 10⁻²¹. The model's convolved form (MATH §1.3, variance sd² + 1/(4n)) reproduces the lattice sum to 0.11% everywhere, so the error is understood; what MATH §1.3 does not say is that its "2% once n sd² > 6.25" holds at mean ½ and the error grows with z. A line in MATH would close it; no p on the site is affected. (A forecast truncated to (0, 1) and renormalized, which the model does not do, would raise the far seats' p by 1/mass: 1.31 for 2018-house-NY-15 at mean 0.96; 1.000 for every p ≥ 10⁻⁶ except by 10⁻⁴.)

### 2.2 Item 2: the second stage

**The College.** D1's W is a 401-point grid over the national swing with the other units' electoral votes counted by convolution of their win chances. `c2_college.py` counts them instead by a Fourier (characteristic-function) product on the unit circle with prefix and suffix products, so that each unit's "others" distribution comes from one pass, on a 1,201-point Simpson grid over ± 12 s_d; and, unrelated to both, by importance-sampling Monte Carlo (10⁶ draws of the national swing and the state errors with the unit held at the tie, the window weight averaged). Fourier agrees with the record to 3 × 10⁻¹², which is what two convergent quadratures of a smooth integrand should give; Monte Carlo agrees within its standard error for all 573 unit-years (largest |z| 2.47; about 0.1 beyond 3 se expected by chance, 0 seen). The districts of 2024 (ME-1, ME-2, NE-1, NE-2, NE-3) are in the count with their own electoral vote and sd. Under 538's marginals the same, with the national swing's sd from the model's own year, which is what the record's method text says and what the code does [measured]. The Economist's 204 window counts are recounted exactly from the raw file.

One finding (2.4): where the Economist's window holds a dozen weighted draws (OH 13, IA 12, CO 28), W depends on the window's width by factors of 3 to 10. The record marks a W as thin only under 10 weighted draws, so OH and IA are not marked. That is a labelling question, not an error: the record says what it counted.

**The chambers.** `c3_chambers.py` rebuilds each chamber-year's inputs from the files (every seat's mean, whether modelled, inferred or fixed; the seats not up; the pivots), and counts the other seats by the same Fourier product on a Simpson grid, with the swing given the tie as MATH §4.2 writes it, d | tie ~ N((½ − μ) s_a²/sd², s_a² s_e²/sd²). The pivots are read from the record (House 217 of the others; Senate 49 or 50 less the Democrats not up and the fixed seats, with the Vice President at ½ in presidential years, as the record now stands). Every Senate seat agrees to 10⁻¹², every House seat to 3 × 10⁻⁷ (the House's sums run over 400 seats and 10⁻⁶ was the tolerance set); the unconditional P(decides), recorded for comparison, to 4 × 10⁻¹⁴. Monte Carlo (2 × 10⁶ draws per Senate year, 4 × 10⁵ for the House of 2018, 2022 and 2024) agrees within its error for every seat. *Not checked:* the `P_decides_given_tie_if_VP_D` and `_if_VP_R` fields; when D2's Vice-President change lands, the presidential-year Senate seats' P(decides | tie) change and `c3` should be rerun on them.

**538 × own (2.6).** Under 538 the control p is 538's seat p times P(decides | tie), and P conditions the national swing on a tie at the **model's own** mean for the seat, with the other seats the model's own. Arithmetically this is exactly what the record says (1.10). As a model it holds two forecasts of the same seat at once: the chance of a tie is 538's, but what a tie *tells* the chamber is the model's. Where the two means agree it is harmless; where 538 puts a seat at 0.48 and the model at 0.33 (2018-house-WV-03), a tie is a small surprise to 538 and a large swing to the model, and P is 0.006 as recorded against 0.023 conditioned on 538's mean. Over all seats the medians are near one (table 2.6), the extremes are factors, and the sum of control p moves by −30% (House 2020, reading c) to +11% (House 2018, reading b). *Two readings.* (a) Keep it as recorded and say so in the method text (the record does): a published seat forecast through the model's chamber stage. (b) or (c) Condition on 538's mean, or take 538's mean and sd as the seat's marginal with s_e,i = √(sd₅₃₈² − s_a²), as the presidency already treats 538's marginals; this makes the seat and the chamber see one world, at the price of mixing 538's one-seat sd with the model's correlation. Reading (c) is the presidency's rule; it is the one that moves most.

### 2.3 Item 3: the forecasts

**The governors.** D2's arithmetic holds: under the record's p (the site's mix, 152 own and 66 538-first), 4.38 governor races were expected within 5,000 votes and none came, P 0.013. The explanation offered, "the forecast runs too wide in the middle so p is too high", fails on both counts. First, the forecast is not too wide in the middle in share: counting races by |share − ½| ≤ h gives expected/seen of 3.7/3, 7.5/6, 14.9/17, 29.7/34, 72.6/82 for h from a quarter point to five points, so the record has, if anything, slightly *more* near-even races than forecast at every width above half a point. Second, a forecast that is too wide has too little density at its centre, so a too-wide forecast gives p too *low*; narrowing it (A3) raises p by 4%. The forecast is about 5% too wide overall (z sd 0.95), not in the middle particularly (the band ratios are 0.96, 0.94, 0.95 for |mean − ½| up to 10 points and 0.86 beyond it). What is left is the vote count: 14 races finished within one point of even, and with 180,000 to a million voters each, none happened to land within 5,000 votes; the nearest are Connecticut 2010 (6,404), Montana 2012 (7,571), Rhode Island 2006 (7,803) and Minnesota 2010 (8,770). Given those 14 close races, 2.0 would be expected within 5,000 and the chance of none is 0.13. The line at 5,000 is the unlucky one: at 10,000 votes 4 were seen against 8.8 (P 0.064), at 20,000 13 against 17.5 (P 0.17). The 66 races where 538 stands first contribute the other part: 538's own forecasts for the governors of 2018 and 2022 ran wide (z sd 0.78) [measured], which lowers their p, not raises it.

*Options, with what each does to p (none applied, each leave-one-cycle-out like the record):* A1 t4 tails at the same sd: p × 0.997 in the median but 0.63 to 1.32 across races, and the 50% interval worse (0.54), so a worse forecast; A2 an error sd that varies with |mean − ½|: z sd 1.000 and p × 1.046 (0.96–1.12), the cleanest calibration; A3 the sd scaled by the back-test's 0.93: z sd 1.000, p × 1.039 (0.86–1.08), the simplest; A4 a lagged term, the state's last governor race, which D2's method lacks: on the 187 races of 2010–2024 that have one, b_lag = +0.25, s_e 0.0666 against 0.0702 without it on the same rows, log score 1.064 → 1.143, p × 1.030 (0.60–1.32), the most informative and the one that changes the method rather than its scale. Every option raises the expected count within 5,000 votes (4.1–4.2) because every one sharpens the forecast; none makes 0 of 4.4 likelier. I recommend nothing; A4 is the only one that adds information, and A2 or A3 the only ones that fix the calibration figure the report quotes.

**Leave one cycle out.** The record fits each cycle on the other nine, so a 2014 forecast uses 2016–2024's coefficients and national misses. `c4_congress.py` refits on earlier cycles only (an expanding window from 2014, the first year with four earlier cycles for the national swing's three terms), with the national sd both as the record has it and from the earlier cycles alone. The table at 3.4 gives the ratio of the sums of the model's own p. Early cycles move most (House 2014 × 0.81, 2016 × 0.86; Senate 2016 × 0.89; governor 2014 × 0.89), because the early fits have wider s_e (House 0.0568 against 0.0489) and the coefficients drift (b_lean 0.77 → 0.81); 2022 is within 3% and 2024 is the same forecast by construction, since leaving 2024 out *is* a fit on the earlier cycles. The site p affected are the model's-own ones: House 2014 (358), 2016 (343), 2024 (373, unchanged), Senate 2024 (32, unchanged), governor 2014 (33). *Two readings:* leave-one-out is the record's standard and uses what the data hold; earlier-only is what a forecaster then could have fitted, and gives p lower by up to a fifth in 2014–2016 and higher by up to 4% for governors in 2018–2022. The orchestrator marked this as the maker's to correct; the sizes are now known. (D2's own test of an expanding window and its finding that it "runs too wide in its early years" is confirmed: the same sums, lower.)

**The presidency's horizon and scale.** `c4b_president_538.py` retypes D1's fit from the record's averages and results: for each year, the national error sd is the root-mean-square miss of the earlier cycles' averages at the same horizon, and the state sd that of the uniform swing's state misses; every year reproduces the record's s_nat and s_state exactly (largest difference 0). For 2024 the input is 538's average of 12 September, the last reachable, and the errors are fitted to the 9 September averages of 1976–2020: a horizon 3 days longer than the input's, which is as close as the record's sources allow; the eve-fitted sd (0.0190) belongs to eve inputs that do not exist for 2024, and using it would raise Pennsylvania's f(½) by 18% while W moved too. The 12 September average missed the result by −0.84 national sd, an ordinary miss. The t4 scale is sd/√2 in both pipeline formulas, retyped and checked against scipy at 10⁻¹⁶, so the t4 has the stated sd; the other readings (scale = sd; a normal) would give 0.83 and 0.90 of the record's f(½).

**538's implied sd.** The run counts inferred from the chances' step are right for every model-year (3.7); for Senate 2016 the only rows off the 20,000 step are the "US" control-of-the-chamber rows, which are not seat chances, so the record's 20,000 stands; Senate 2014's chances have no step at all (20 decimals), and the record takes them as exact with no sampling error, which is right if 538's 2014 model was analytic and unknowable otherwise; the 34 entries stand first on that reading. The error formula reproduces to the record's rounding (3.8). The rule itself guards the sd's relative error, which is the quantity the 20% is stated on. But p = φ(z)/(sd × 2n) depends on sd through both the density and the scale, and its relative error is |z² − 1| times the sd's: at z = 1 p does not depend on sd at all; at z = 3.94 (2018 House lite entries at 538's rounding of 0.0 or 100.0%) a 7% sd error is a 105% p error. Over the 1,320 entries the site shows first, 174 exceed 20% on p (House 155, presidency 10, Senate 7, governor 2), 25 exceed 100% (22 House, 2 presidency, 1 Senate). The 190 entries the record rejects would all be rejected by a p rule too. *Two readings:* keep the sd rule, which is what the record states and is honest about the sd; or state the rule on p's error, which is what the reader sees, and the 174 entries would fall to the model's own forecast (which stands beside them in the files already). Either is defensible; the second changes 174 site figures.

### 2.4 Item 4: the published figures

**The Economist, 2020.** The Economist does not state its method for `p_decisive_vote` [stated, D1], and its public repository (157 files at commit b89e602) holds no code for it [measured]. Two things explain the spread. (i) *Which estimator.* The record counts ties in a ± 0.01 window of the share and weights by whether the unit decides: an empirical count. GSE's method (model §4; MATH §3.4) multiplies P(the state is necessary) by the density at zero of a t4 fitted to the vote margin among the necessary draws. `c5_published.py` runs GSE's method on the Economist's own draws: it gives 0.903 of the published figure in the median over all 51 states (0.35–1.60; within 25% in 30), the same ratio the record's window has over its 16, and it runs at 0.86–0.98 of the window's own count where the window holds hundreds of draws (PA 0.98, MI 0.86, FL 0.91, AZ 0.89, GA 0.89, NC 0.89, TX 0.90), the t4 fit's shape being a little flatter at zero than the draws. Where the window holds a dozen draws the two part: for Ohio the window counts 13 weighted draws (6.9 × 10⁻¹⁰), the density among the necessary draws is 6.7 × 10⁻¹⁰ by a local count, and a t4 *fitted* to those draws puts 1.76 × 10⁻⁹ at zero because the fit's tail is heavier than the draws' own; the published figure is 4.98 × 10⁻⁹. So Ohio's 0.14 is the gap between an empirical count and a fitted tail in a state 3.4 sd from necessity, where the draws cannot say which is right. (ii) *Which n.* At the level, both the window and GSE-t4 sit at about 0.90 of the published figure; if the Economist's method is GSE's, the n it implies is 0.82 of the 2020 count and 0.97 of the 2016 count in the median, which is consistent with the Economist having used 2016-sized turnout [inferred; the Economist does not say]. (iii) *What remains.* In Wisconsin, New Hampshire, Minnesota and Nevada both estimators sit at 0.62–0.68 (GSE-t4) and 0.67–0.92 (the window) of the published figure, and in Arizona at 1.18 and 1.32; these windows hold 135 to 600 weighted draws, so sampling error (4–9%) does not cover it, and no reading of these draws I have tried (window widths, a local-linear fit, GSE with normal or t4 tails at either scale) reproduces them. The published figure is then not a function of these 80,000 draws by either method, which points to another draw set or another n per state (turnout by state is the one input neither method here varies). *Likelier right:* for the site's definition — the chance of a tie by the draws' own count, with the certified n — the record's figure, whose counts are exact. The Economist's is a different estimator with an unstated n and, it seems, other draws; it is not wrong, it is a different question. *What would settle it:* the Economist's script for `p_decisive_vote`, or its per-state n and draw set.

**GKB 1992.** `c5` runs the record's 1992 College stage under other inputs. The record's forecast is the eve average (0.5467), s_nat 0.022, s_state 0.0425 (t4): it gives the Democrat 0.956 of the College, so the College is rarely close and the average voter's p is 1 in 45.6 million. GKB's errors (Table 1 of the paper, as the ledger B4 records them: national 2.5%, regional 2.5%, state 3.5%) on the same mean give 1 in 30.9 million; the 9 September average and its fit (s_nat 0.035) 1 in 29.5 million; and a mean nearer even (0.52, which is not in any source) 1 in 13 million. So the method is not the gap; the forecast is: GKB forecast earlier in the year, with wider errors and a closer race, and such a forecast gives about 1 in 30 million; "about 1 in 20 million" needs their state means too. *Likelier right:* each for its own inputs; the site's figure is the model's with the eve forecast, and that is what the record says it is. *What would settle it:* GKB's state means (their forecast table), which the record does not hold.

**GSE 2008.** GSE's forecast was Silver's of 24 October 2008 from state polls, with their own turnout; the record's is the uniform swing from the 3 November national average with s_state 0.037. The nearest poll-based 2008 forecast on hand is the Economist's 2008 back-test (its README's table, read as recorded): its means miss the results by 0.016 on average against the uniform swing's 0.027, and its state sds are about half the record's. That is the whole gap: in the close states a better-informed mean and a narrower error put more density at even, and in California a narrower error puts far less (the record's CA is 9 sd from even under s_state 0.037 and much further under 0.02). Through the record's own College stage, the own means with a poll-sized state sd give NM 1 in 12.9M, OH 12.6M and CA 970M, close to GSE's "about 1 in 10 million" and "about 1 in a billion"; the Economist's election-day means give the Democrat 1.000 of the College, so no national sd fits them and the close states come out *lower* (NM 65M). *Likelier right:* GSE's, for 2008 as a forecast made on 24 October from the state polls; the record's, for what the model says with the inputs it has (no polls, uniform swing). The site shows the model's and says so. *What would settle it:* Silver's 24 October state means and sds, which are not reachable (D1's unread list).

### 2.5 Item 5: the record against the forecasts (presidency)

`c6_record.py` does for the presidency what D2 did for Congress: under the forecast each p used, the expected number of contests decided by k votes or fewer, against the number seen. In the cdf form (the forecast's mass over the band, which is exact for the normal and t4 forecasts and for the convolution) the expected / seen are 0.002 / 0 at k = 1, 0.02 / 0 at 10, 0.20 / 0 at 100, 1.98 / 2 at 1,000, 9.9 / 7 at 5,000, 19.7 / 15 at 10,000, 39.3 / 34 at 20,000, 97.1 / 98 at 50,000 and 187.6 / 189 at 100,000; the Poisson chance of the count seen or fewer is at least 0.17 at every k, and of the count seen or more at least 0.47. The forecasts pass. The two within 1,000 votes are New Mexico 2000 (366) and Florida 2000 (537). Under each forecast separately (538's three 2016 models, polls-plus over 2016–2020, the Economist's 16, the model's own 566) the same picture. One note: D2's test uses the density form (expected = 2pk), which is right for k up to about 20,000 for the presidency (within 0.4%) and understates the expected count at 50,000 and 100,000 (89 and 149 against 97 and 188), because f varies across so wide a band; MATH §8.2 should say the cdf form for large k.
