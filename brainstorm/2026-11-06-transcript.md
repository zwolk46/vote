**Orchestrator, 19:34.** Got it: "importance" means the chance your one vote changes the winner, with everything else held fixed (no persuasion, no duty, no one seeing you). That is the classic setup in political science, so there is a standard of review to borrow:

**Expected benefit = p × B, weighed against C.**
- **p**: the chance your vote is decisive, meaning without it the count would be an exact tie or your side one short.
- **B**: how much better the outcome you vote for is.
- **C**: what voting costs you (time, travel, effort).

What drives p: the number of voters, how close the race is expected to be, how uncertain that forecast is, and the structure (in a presidential race your vote must flip your state, and your state must flip the Electoral College). Small, close, uncertain races have far higher p than big, lopsided ones. There are published estimates of p for real elections and records of races actually decided by one vote; I'll read them at their sources before we lean on any figure.

The question that matters most for your claim: does B count only what the outcome does for you, or also what it does for everyone else it affects? If it counts others, B grows with the population about as fast as p shrinks, which is the argument some economists make for why the product doesn't vanish. If only you, your claim is very hard to beat in large elections.
