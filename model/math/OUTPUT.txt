5.1 Section 2: 'the expected outcome when you vote, minus when you don't, equals p times B, and nothing else'
    E[gain] = P(M=0) (U_A - U_tie) + P(M=-1) (U_tie - U_B). With B = U_A - U_B this is p B exactly when
    U_tie = (U_A + U_B)/2, i.e. when a tie settled by a fair lot is valued at its expectation; and for any
    deterministic tie rule (only one term is non-zero and U_tie is U_A or U_B). Numerically, n = 1000 and 1001:
    n=1000: U_A=1, U_B=0, U_tie=0.5: E[gain] = 0.006363; p B = 0.006363; ratio 1.000
    n=1000: U_A=1, U_B=0, U_tie=0.3: E[gain] = 0.008908; p B = 0.006363; ratio 1.400
    n=1001: U_A=1, U_B=0, U_tie=0.5: E[gain] = 0.006321; p B = 0.006321; ratio 1.000
    n=1001: U_A=1, U_B=0, U_tie=0.3: E[gain] = 0.003792; p B = 0.006321; ratio 0.600
    Verdict: holds under the stated condition (a tie is worth the average of the two outcomes, or the
    jurisdiction's rule is deterministic); otherwise a tie-value term is added. With parity unknown ex ante
    the two cases average to p B for any tie value.

5.2 Section 2 arithmetic: B* = C / p
    p = 1e-07 (1 in 10,000,000), C = $4: B* = $40,000,000
    p = 1e-07 (1 in 10,000,000), C = $12: B* = $120,000,000
    p = 1.67e-08 (1 in 60,000,000), C = $4: B* = $240,000,000
    p = 1.67e-08 (1 in 60,000,000), C = $12: B* = $720,000,000
    Verdict: '$40 to $120 million' holds; '$230 to $720 million' should read $240 to $720 million
    (C = $4 at p = 1 in 60 million gives $240 million; $230 million needs C = $3.83).

5.3 Section 3: 'with a fair coin breaking ties, p = 1/2 x [chance of an exact tie + chance your side is one short]'
    Holds under a fair lot. At a given n only one of the two chances is non-zero (an exact tie needs n even,
    one short needs n odd); so p = (1/2) P(tie) or (1/2) P(one short), never a sum of two non-zero terms.
    Under a deterministic tie rule the formula is wrong for either parity (p = 0 or p = P(event)); see S1.1.
    'Either way, your ballot moves the result half a win': holds under the lot (W rises by 1/2 in each event).

5.4 Section 3: 'the 1/2 appears in Gelman, King & Boscardin (1998) and Barnett (2020)'
    Barnett 2020: yes, EB = (1/2) B d, d the exact-tie event. GKB 1998: yes in the section-5 rough formula
    ('for casting a vote, not switching a preference'); no in the model of eqs. 1-3, whose state-level term
    is the event probability f/n and whose 1/2 weights the Electoral-College tie ends. Verdict: holds in part.

5.5 Section 3: 'p ~ (how likely the race is to land at dead even) / (number of voters), up to a small constant'
    Holds with the constant stated: p = f(1/2)/(2n) with f the forecast density of the share at one half, or
    p = 25 q1 / n with q1 = P(share within one point of even). Without the constant, 'closeness / voters' is
    off by a factor that depends on what 'closeness' means (S6.4). The error of the approximation is
    +1/(8 n tau^2) at mu = 1/2 (S1.3): small once n tau^2 exceeds ~10.

     where recount zones are a few hundred votes against forecast spreads of tens of thousands.

5.11 Section 2: 'p is the chance that your ballot changes the winner'
     P(the declared winner differs with and without your ballot) = sum_M P(M) P(differs | M). Under a lot the
     coin that decides a tie is the same coin either way, so at M = 0 the winner differs iff the coin would
     have gone to B (1/2), and at M = -1 iff it now goes to A (1/2): (1/2)P(M=0) + (1/2)P(M=-1) = p. Under a
     deterministic rule the winner differs iff the one event occurs, = p. Verdict: holds (with the coin
     coupled across the two worlds; any other coupling gives a larger 'differs' probability and p is the
     rise in A's chance, which is what the decision needs).

5.12 Sections 2 and 8: '1 in 10 million' (best-placed 2008 states) and '1 in 60 million' (average 2008 voter)
     are Gelman, Silver & Edlin's figures. As written in their text (the density at 0 of the margin in votes)
     they are in our convention; if an exact-tie probability was intended they are twice ours (S2.5). Their
     scale convention adds a factor of up to sqrt(2) either way at the centre. Verdict: usable as ours, with
     that caveat stated beside them; B* = C/p then holds as computed in 5.2.

5.13 Section 8: 'the arithmetic is one line: p ~ closeness / voters, and B* = C / p'
     Holds with the constant of 5.5 and the two-stage factor of 5.9 where a state or a seat feeds a larger
     body; the simple mode's error is bounded in S6.2 (within a factor 1.2 of the exact p for n >= 1,000 and
     forecast sd >= 2 points; within 1.03 for n >= 10,000 and sd >= 3 points).

7.3 Plurality, three candidates, Dirichlet forecast (kappa = 400): the continuous route.
    Candidate 1 can tie with either rival j. For the pair (1, j): s = v1 + vj ~ Beta(kappa(mu1+muj), kappa mu_k)
    and r = v1/(v1+vj) ~ Beta(kappa mu1, kappa muj), independent; D = v1 - vj = s(2r - 1) has density
    f_D(0) = f_r(1/2) E[1[s > 2/3]/(2s)] (the third must be below the tied pair, 1 - s < s/2). Q1 ~ sum_j f_D(0)/n,

7.6 A turnout quorum (STRUCTURE, with a stylised number). Let the proposal fail unless turnout T >= q R
    (R registered). A ballot can change the outcome in two ways: the ordinary tie among those who vote, and
    completing the quorum when T would have fallen one short. Ex ante, with f_T the density of the turnout
    share at q (over R) and P(pass | quorum met) the chance the vote itself passes:
      Q_yes ~ f_v(1/2)/(2N) x P(quorum met | tie)  +  f_T(q)/R x P(pass | quorum met, T = qR)
      Q_no  ~ f_v(1/2)/(2N) x P(quorum met | tie)  -  f_T(q)/R x P(pass | quorum met, T = qR)   (a No ballot that
              completes the quorum HELPS the proposal; a No voter who wants it to fail should abstain).
    Stylised: R = 1,000,000, turnout forecast 52% (sd 3), q = 50%; Yes share forecast 56% (sd 4), P(pass | quorum) = 0.93:
      tie term f_v(1/2)/(2N) = 3.113e-06; quorum term f_T(q)/R x 0.93 = 9.903e-06: the quorum term is 3.2 x the tie term.
    No published counterpart was read; R4 gave structure only, and so does this.

7.8 Runoff, IRV, STV: R4 made no calculation, and reports Baltz's closed forms as unreliable against exact
    counts (1/2 to 1/21 of exact). Structure only: a two-round runoff gives p = p(first round settles it or
    sets the pair) + sum over pairs P(pair) x p(final | pair); IRV sums over elimination orders; STV over
    quotas and transfers. Each term is a two-way p of the kind in S1 with the pair's conditional forecast, so
    the model can compute them by enumeration for small N and by simulation otherwise; nothing here is
    offered as the model's own number.

7.9 Chamber: R4's chamber.py reports P(other 434 seats give exactly 217) without conditioning on the tied
    seat (its 'P(tip | seat tied)' is the unconditional P(217)); S4.4 shows the conditional one. Its magnitude
    (1 in 39 to 1 in 93 at m0 = 0) agrees with S4.4's unconditional column (0.015 to 0.022 at rho 0.3-0.7).
    Its rows are a function of three random draws of 434 seat means (seed 0, 1, 2), as R4's note says.

7.10 Verdicts: sound enough for the model's own use: two-way exact (7.7), plurality-3 (7.2, 7.3), list PR as
     'about M/N' when the forecast spans a seat (7.4), referendum thresholds with N averaged (7.5). Structure
     only, no number: quorum (7.6), runoff/IRV/STV (7.8). Chamber: use the conditional form (7.9, S4.4).

8.2 What a per-ballot frequency estimates. Let election e have N_e voters and forecast density f_e of the
    share at one half. Ex ante, with parity unknown: P(exact tie) ~ f_e/(2 N_e), P(one-vote win) ~ f_e/N_e,
    and our p_e = f_e/(2 N_e), the event (tie or one short) ~ f_e/N_e.
    'Ballots cast for a candidate who tied or won by one' count N_e in a tie and ~N_e/2 in a one-vote win, so
    E[count_e] ~ N_e [f_e/(2N_e)] + (N_e/2)[f_e/N_e] = f_e. The frequency F = sum count_e / sum N_e therefore
    estimates sum_e f_e / sum_e N_e = the ballot-weighted average of the EVENT probability f_e/N_e,
    which is twice the ballot-weighted average of our p_e. Counting every ballot in a pivot election gives
    1.5 sum f_e / sum N_e (M&H's eq. 4), three times the average p.
    US '1 in 89,252' (winner's ballots): implied ballot-weighted average p = F/2 = 5.602e-06 = 1 in 178,504
    state '1 in 15,444' (all ballots): implied ballot-weighted average p = F/3 = 2.158e-05 = 1 in 46,333
    state in the US treatment, '1 in 25,527': implied ballot-weighted average p = F/2 = 1.959e-05 = 1 in 51,055
    Implied average density at one half: US f-bar = F x average N = 1.25; state f-bar = 1.02
    (M&H's own Figure 5 reading is f ~ 2 for the whole sample; GKB's '18% of states within 2 points' is
    f ~ 4.5 for presidential-state forecasts: House and state-legislative races are on average less close.)

8.3 The sampling error of a count of one
    US House one-vote races: 1 in 16,577; 95% interval for the expected count [0.025, 5.57], i.e. the rate
      is known to within a factor 5.6 above and 39.5 below the point estimate
    state legislative ties or one-vote races: 9 in 40,036; 95% interval for the expected count [4.115, 17.08], i.e. the rate
      is known to within a factor 1.9 above and 2.2 below the point estimate
    So '1 in 89,252' is a point estimate from one event; the data are consistent with 1 in 16,019 and with 1 in 3,525,271.

8.4 Klarner's counts (state legislative general elections 1968-2022; R2, section 1b)
    all contests with a margin: 52 events (margin 0 or 1) in 109,093: 0.0004767 per contest (1 in 2,098); 95% [3.56e-04, 6.25e-04]
    single-seat contests: 30 events (margin 0 or 1) in 99,165: 0.0003025 per contest (1 in 3,306); 95% [2.04e-04, 4.32e-04]
    2018-2024 (seats): 5 events (margin 0 or 1) in 23,830: 0.0002098 per contest (1 in 4,766); 95% [6.81e-05, 4.90e-04]
    A per-CONTEST event rate estimates the contest-average of P(margin 0 or 1) = 1.5 f_e/N_e (parity averaged),
    i.e. 3 x the contest-average of p_e. Contest averages weight small districts heavily (p_e ~ 1/N_e), so
    the implied contest-average p = 0.0001589 (1 in 6,294) is not comparable to M&H's
    ballot-weighted figure, which weights large districts. Both are averages over every contest, lopsided
    ones included; neither is the p of any particular contest.

8.5 Relation to the ex ante p, in one line each
    (i) F estimates an average of event probabilities over a population of elections, not a probability for one.
    (ii) The ex ante p for one election can be far from that average: 1/(2N) x f_e with f_e from 0 (a 70-30
         race) to ~20 (a dead heat with a 2-point sd); the population average f-bar is about 1 to 2.
    (iii) F counts the first official margin; later counts and recounts move it (M&H's own check cut their
          unweighted frequency by 50% and the vote-weighted by 76%), so F overstates how often a ballot settled
          the result under section 7's smoothed definition.
    (iv) The reality check the model can pass: the sum over a population of elections of the model's
         event probabilities should land inside the Poisson interval of the observed count. For Klarner's
         universe that is 52 events with 95% interval [39, 68]; the check needs a forecast
         for each contest, which no source read provides. What can be checked now is the average density:
         F x N-bar = 1.25 (US) and 1.02 (state), against ~2 from M&H's own Figure 5.
