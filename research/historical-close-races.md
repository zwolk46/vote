## 1. Empirical studies of how often an election is decided by one vote

### 1a. Mulligan & Hunter (the study the brief names)

- **Citation.** Casey B. Mulligan and Charles G. Hunter, "The Empirical Frequency of a Pivotal Vote," *Public Choice* 116(1-2): 31-54, 2003 [stated via WebFetch: https://ideas.repec.org/a/kap/pubcho/v116y2003i1-2p31-54.html and https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:kap:pubcho:v:116:y:2003:i:1-2:p:31-54 (no DOI listed there)]. Working-paper version: NBER Working Paper 8590, November 2001, DOI 10.3386/w8590 [stated via WebFetch: https://www.nber.org/papers/w8590]. The paper itself I read (as WebFetch answers) from https://www.nber.org/papers/w8590.pdf; the Springer page was refused. **The published and working versions differ in the US headline number** (section 6).
- **Abstract headline, as quoted.** Published (IDEAS/EconPapers): "One of every 89,000 votes cast in U.S. Congressional elections" and "one of 15,000 in state legislator elections"; recounts, "and other margin-specific election procedures, are determinants of the pivotal vote frequency." Working paper (NBER PDF abstract): "one of every 100,000 votes cast in U.S. elections, and one of every 15,000 votes cast in state elections"; "Very close elections are more rare than the independent binomial model predicts"; "recounts, and other margin-specific election procedures, are quite relevant determinants of the frequency of a pivotal vote."
- **Datasets** [stated via WebFetch, NBER PDF Sec. I]: US House returns "for the election years 1898-1992" (ICPSR study 6311), state legislative returns "for the years 1968-89" (ICPSR study 8907). US: an "exceptions file" of 839 elections omitted and 3,228 more dropped for missing, zero or uncontested votes, "leaving 16577 US returns for analysis." State: multi-member free-for-all districts (5,785), missing votes (2,478) and uncontested (11,047, plus 179 de facto uncontested) excluded, "This leaves 40,036 elections." The state sample has "almost one billion votes"; average votes per election in Table 1: 111,370 (US), 23,658 (state).
- **Counts** [stated via WebFetch, Sec. II.C-D]:
  - US House: "Of the 16577 US returns analyzed, only one (1/16577=0.00006) was decided by a single vote. Two others were decided by 4 votes, one by 5, and two by 9 votes." "there were no US elections that tied, or were decided by 2, 3, 6, 7, or 8 votes."
  - State legislatures: "two were tied and seven were decided by a single vote"; "61,328 votes were cast in those nine elections"; "only 29 were decided by 1-4 votes."
  - [measured] The state figure is 9 pivot elections in 40,036 = 2.2 x 10^-4 per election (about 1 in 4,400); the US figure 6.0 x 10^-5 (1 in 16,577). The paper's Section IV.A gives an unweighted frequency of 0.0002 that falls to 0.0001 when later counts replace official ones.
- **Definitions** [stated via WebFetch, Sec. II.B-C]: a pivot election is one where "the winner's official and final vote total exceeded the loser's official and final vote total by no more than one vote"; a pivotal vote is one "cast for the winner in a pivot election, or cast for either candidate in a tied election"; ties are weighted by assuming "the probability of a tied election is half of that" of a one-vote margin. The per-vote (vote-weighted) figure p is "the probability that a randomly chosen ballot is cast in an election decided by zero or one votes." Estimates come from a margin-based model, not a bare division of counts.
- **Size relation** [stated via WebFetch]: the frequency "is closely approximated by 2/v, which is broadly consistent with a binomial model" (Sec. V, p. 26) for state elections; "smaller U.S. Congressional elections are an interesting exception to the 2/v rule"; "2/v seems to be somewhat less than a reasonably calibrated binomial model would predict"; for state elections of size v = 20,000 "Figure 5 shows the frequency of a pivot election ... to be 1/6000." Table 2 (state, medium tertile, 9,183-21,398 votes): q(4) 2.7 x 10^-4, p(4) 2.4 x 10^-4 (the source's column headers were garbled; check against the PDF).
- **Recounts and "final" counts** [stated via WebFetch, Sec. III.B, IV.A, V]: the authors checked the ten elections coded tied or one-vote in newspapers (Table 3). Quotes: "the procedure for determining the winner may be flexible and margin-specific"; later counts "typically reveal a larger margin of victory than officially recorded"; "the official counts are not adjusted when the winner does not change"; using the latest counts the unweighted frequency (Sec. IV.A) falls from 0.0002 to 0.0001 ("This is a 50% reduction") and the vote-weighted frequency by about 76%; "one cannot know for sure whether or not the outcome hinged on one vote"; if officially close elections were always recounted "the frequency of a pivotal ballot would only be 1/v, rather than 2/v." They add that "Exhaustive legal research on the conduct of close elections is beyond the scope of our paper" and mention a Rhode Island rerun.
- **Fit for the site** [inferred]: this is the one peer-reviewed count of one-vote and tied outcomes over tens of thousands of races, it covers 1898-1992 (House) and 1968-89 (states) only, and it defines the pivot on the official final count, which the authors themselves show is not fixed.

### 1c. Other empirical work read

- **Eggers, Folke, Fowler, Hainmueller, Hall and Snyder**, "On the Validity of the Regression Discontinuity Design for Estimating Electoral Effects: New Evidence from Over 40,000 Close Races," MIT Political Science Department Working Paper 2013-26 (June 2013) [stated via WebFetch: https://jonnyphillips.github.io/FLS6415/Class_7/Eggers.pdf]. "This sample includes a broad range of politically significant national and local elections from 10 different countries" (US House, statewide, state legislative, mayoral; Canada, New Zealand, Germany, India, Brazil, Mexico, others). Table 2 pooled: 41,124 races within a 10% margin, 8,463 within 2%, 4,212 within 1%. The paper does not mention ties or exact one-vote margins. Sources named: ICPSR Study 34297 (US state legislatures), the Constituency-Level Elections Archive (Canada, NZ), the Federal Returning Officer (Germany), the Election Commission of India, Brazil's Superior Electoral Tribunal. [measured] The counts grow almost in proportion to width (4,212 -> 8,463 is x2.01; 8,463 -> 41,124 is x4.86 for a x5 window): the density of margins near zero is roughly flat out to 10% [inferred]. Use: a margin-density shape for the model; not a one-vote count.
- **Gelman, Silver and Edlin**, "What Is the Probability Your Vote Will Make a Difference?" *Economic Inquiry* 50(2): 321-326 (2012) [stated via WebFetch: https://sites.stat.columbia.edu/gelman/research/published/probdecisive2.pdf]. Quotes: "Elections are very rarely tied, so one can instead use an estimate such as y/(2,000 n), where y is now the number of elections decided by less than 1,000 votes" (a count-based estimator); "the complex structure and the small number of presidential elections rule out a purely empirical approach in this case"; 2008: "approximate 1 in 10 million chance of determining the national election outcome" in the closest states and "On average, a voter in America had a 1 in 60 million chance." (The model side is another subagent's; recorded here because the empirical estimator bears on section 1.)

## 3. What happens at a tie or near-tie

### 3a. Tie-breaking rules by state (NCSL)

### 3c. How the very close races were actually settled

From 2a: in each of the ten verified cases a process after the first count (recount, rulings on particular ballots, a court or the legislature) fixed, changed or voided the result; in nine of the ten the first announced margin differed from the final one (Winchester's first margin was not in the source). The deciding actor was, case by case, a canvassing board counting or excluding specific ballots (MN 2008), a recount (AK HD1 2018, WA 2004), a three-judge panel adding one ballot (VA HD94), a state supreme court (MN 2008, AK 2019, IN 2026, Bush v. Gore), a legislative committee (KY 2018, IA-2 2020), a lot (VA HD94) or the threat of a suit (KY HD13), a clerical or label error that voided a one-vote federal result (Canada 2026). [inferred] In the US, with a margin of one, the result is decided by (a) the margin that survives a recount at the margin-trigger thresholds in 3b, (b) rulings on a handful of disputed ballots (the Alaska judge examined nine; the Minnesota courts counted 933 absentee ballots; Virginia turned on one filled-in bubble), and (c) tie-break rules in 3a. The counted "one vote" is not the decider; it is one input to those processes.

## 4. Accuracy of counting: is noise larger than one vote?

- **FairVote statewide recounts** [stated via WebFetch]. (i) "Statewide Election Recounts, 2000-2015" (Nov 29, 2016; https://fairvote.org/report/recounts/): "Out of the 4,687 statewide general elections in the 2000 to 2015 decade, there were 27 statewide recounts"; "three reversals out of 15 consequential recounts" (original margin "no more than 0.15 percent"); "an average margin swing of 282 votes between the frontrunners"; "The median average shift was 219 votes"; "22 of the 27 recounts changing the victory margin by fewer than 500 votes." (iii) A later FairVote analysis reposted on Election Law Blog (https://electionlawblog.org/?p=146675, no date in the text read): "Out of the 6,929 statewide general elections between 2000 and 2023, there were 36 statewide recounts", "only three reversals, or one out of every 2,310 statewide elections", "an average margin shift of 551 votes between the frontrunners", and "Recounts typically widen the gap between the top two candidates instead of decreasing it." ## 6. Summary, open questions, unread list

### Summary (one paragraph)

Exact ties and one-vote margins are real but rare and cluster in small contests: Mulligan & Hunter (Public Choice 2003) find 1 one-vote US House race in 16,577 (1898-1992) and, in state legislatures 1968-89, 2 ties and 7 one-vote races in 40,036, and they put the per-vote rate at about 1 in 89,000 (published; 1 in 100,000 in the working paper) for Congress and 1 in 15,000 for state legislators; my sum of Ballotpedia's lists for 2018-2024 gives 6 races (1 tie, 5 one-vote) in 24,033 state legislative races (2.5 x 10^-4), and in all offices since 2018 Ballotpedia lists 104 one-vote races of 150,010, almost all with a few dozen to a few hundred votes (largest 23,525 total). No statewide or federal general election read here was decided by one vote: the closest presidential state margins in 2000-2020 were 366 (NM 2000), 537 (FL 2000), 2,736 (NH 2016), 3,903 (MO 2008), 10,457 (AZ 2020) and 11,779 (GA 2020) per the FEC; the closest federal one-vote results found are the 1910 NY-36 House race (Wikipedia) and the 2025 Canadian Terrebonne result (annulled by the Supreme Court of Canada). When the count is that close, the counted margin does not decide: recount triggers (about 21 states automatic at 0.1%-2% or 20-2,000 votes), canvassing boards, three-judge panels and supreme courts (MN 2008 Franken +312 from Coleman +215; WA 2004; VA HD94 2017 decided by a drawn canister after one ballot made a tie; AK HD1 2018; KY HD13 2018; IA-2 2020 margin 6; IN SD23 2026), and tie-break rules (28 states by lot, 14 by new election) do. Counting noise is far above one vote: Minnesota's 2008 recount made a gross change of 2,170 votes in 2.4 million, Georgia's 2020 audit found about 5,000 uncounted ballots, New Hampshire recounts differ from initial counts by about 0.5-0.9% of ballots, and FairVote's statewide recounts shift margins by a median 219 (2000-2015) to a mean 551 (2000-2023) votes. So "decisive by one vote" is well defined only for a stated official count and process, and the empirical frequency of a bare one-vote margin overstates how often one ballot truly settled the outcome (Mulligan & Hunter's later-count check halves it).
