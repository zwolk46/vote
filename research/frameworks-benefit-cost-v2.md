**1.1a Two things the first report said about this table, corrected.**

- The first report wrote that SEP, Geys, Edlin et al. and Dowding all write the structure *probability of decisiveness × benefit differential − cost*, and that "the D term is added after it". **[X]** For SEP that is wrong: its equation has no outcome-independent term. "In contrast, the expressive theory of voting holds that voters vote in order to express themselves and their fidelity to certain groups or ideas." (sec. 1.3). SEP lists expression, consumption value, a perceived duty (Mackie 2010) and causal responsibility (Tuck, Goldman) as separate reasons in prose, and never names any of them D (B1:F2b, N10, N12, N13). **[C]** for Geys: "First, because it still holds that P, and thus PB, is close to zero, equation (2) can be reduced to R = D - C." (printed p. 19; B2:G04). **[X]** for Edlin et al.: D is not added after pB; it is folded into c, whose definition is "the costs, minus the direct benefit of voting", so c can be negative (B3:E05, S9). Other texts that add D: Coate & Conlin ("defines the benefits of voting as pB + d", printed p. 4; B2:T01) and Gibson et al. ("C < PB + D", B2:T02).
- **Notation trap for the site.** In SEP, `D` and `R` are the two candidates; in Geys, Riker & Ordeshook, Coate & Conlin and Gibson et al., `D` (or `d`) is the non-outcome benefit and `R` is the net utility. Anything the site shows from two sources must not mix them (B1:F2b).

### 1.1b What D is: the sources disagree, and Geys is not on the "citizen duty" side

**[X]** The first report set Geys ("benefit from expressing oneself") against Sanders and the arXiv review ("citizen duty"). In Geys the two are the same term: his one gloss of D has two readings, "In the former case, a voter turns out because he or she feels morally obliged to do so (that is, 'civic duty')" and expressing a preference amongst the candidates; the civic-duty reading is inside D (printed p. 19; B2:G07). Read at source, the descriptions of D are:

### 1.1c Whose is `R = PB - C + D`? (the orchestrator's question)

- **Established at second hand, not at source.** Six later texts attribute the added D term to Riker & Ordeshook (1968) and the original `PB - C` to Downs (1957): Geys ("Riker and Ordeshook (1968), for instance, reformulated the original equation into" eq. 2, printed p. 19), the arXiv review ("To overcome this impasse, Riker & Ordeshook (1968), in their study, tried to resolve this problem by adding", sec. 3.1), Coate & Conlin ("The well-known calculus of voting model of turnout (Downs (1957), Riker and Ordeshook (1968)) defines the benefits of voting as pB + d", printed p. 4), Gibson et al., Feddersen & Sandroni and Hamlin & Jennings (B2: T01 to T05, G03, X01).
- **What R&O's own page shows** (Cambridge Core, notes only): they use the letters P, B, C and D; D is tied to the citizen-duty scale (notes 15, 16); "this outcome is worth B utiles" (note 19); C is "the cost of decision in the general election" (note 9); the probability P is subjective (note 10: "since we are dealing with subjective probabilities"). Note 7 says Downs cannot include "non-political" benefits "(i.e. those calculated in (7) but not in (1))", which shows R&O's equation (7) calculates more than the Downs-style equation (1); it does not name D in that note. R&O's equation itself, and whether it is written `PB - C + D` or `PB + D - C`, was **not seen**. **[X]** the first report's "C and V appear as cost and value" is wrong in part: V is never defined in the notes (it appears as `V/2`, a vote threshold) (B2:R04, R05, R06).
- **Conclusion for the record:** the attribution of D to R&O is confirmed by six independent later texts and by R&O's own notes' use of D; the exact form of their formula remains unread.

### 2.2 From B to the expected value of one vote: the tie algebra

1. **[M] The identity, verified by the lead.** Let M be the margin among the others (A's votes minus B's) and let an exact tie be settled by a fair coin. Then P(A wins | you vote A) - P(A wins | you abstain) = ½[P(M = 0) + P(M = -1)]. Reason: abstaining, A wins if M > 0 and half the time if M = 0; voting, A wins outright if M ≥ 0 and half the time if M = -1; the difference is ½P(M = 0) + ½P(M = -1). Checked exactly by computer for three binomial electorates (n = 10, 11, 101): the two sides agree to six digits (for n = 11, θ = 0.528: 0.1049255 on both sides). So the expected value of the vote is `½[P(M=0) + P(M=-1)] × (U_A - U_B)`, as in the first report. **[C]** the algebra; the first report's script is not needed.
2. **Barnett writes the same thing.** **[C]** "a vote that breaks a tie (when the number of voters is odd) or causes a tie (when the number of voters is even)." and "For simplicity, we'll assume that, in the event of an exact tie, both candidates are equally likely to be awarded victory-perhaps a coin is flipped to determine a winner." and "So the expected benefit of a vote for Daisy, then, is ½ × B × d." (printed p. 426; B5:B5-04). Barnett's ½ sits **outside** his d, and d is the probability of the tie-breaking-or-creating event.
3. **[X] "Consistent with GKB's ½" is wrong as the first report put it.** The first report said its p matched "GKB's ½". In Gelman, King & Boscardin (1998) the ½ is on the **national electoral-vote tie only** ("The factors of 1/2 arise because a vote that causes the national election to be tied is only half as decisive", sect. 3.1, eq. (3)); at the state level their eq. ### 3.7 Altruistic / social-welfare voting (Edlin, Gelman & Kaplan; Fowler; Barnett)

- **Edlin, Gelman & Kaplan** (the NBER working paper; section 0.4). **[C]** abstract: "For voters with 'social' preferences, the expected utility of voting is approximately independent of the size of the electorate", and the feedback mechanism sentence (B3:E17). ### 4.1 What the literature counts as cost

- **Geys (2006), printed pp. 17-18, 22.** **[C]** C is split in two. Before election day: "there are costs that a voter makes before the election day (and which are sunk at the time of the election) for, say, gathering information about the candidates, their policy proposals and so on." On election day: "These comprise 'shoe leather' costs to get to the polling station and opportunity costs from the time spent in casting a vote." ## 7. Summary (one paragraph)

Every source I could read writes the calculus of voting as **a probability of decisiveness times a benefit differential, minus a cost**; Geys (`R = PB − C + D`), Coate & Conlin (`pB + d`), Gibson et al. (`C < PB + D`) and Riker & Ordeshook's notes add an outcome-independent term D that is not multiplied by p, SEP (`U_v = p[V(D) − V(R)] − C`, where D is a candidate) has none, and Edlin et al. (`ΔU = pB − c`) fold direct benefits into c; the sources agree D is a benefit that does not come through the outcome and disagree on what it is (duty, expression, consumption), and Riker & Ordeshook's own equation and Downs's text remain unread. With a fair-coin tie rule the expected value of one vote is p × B with `p = ½[P(others tied) + P(your side one short)]` (my algebra, checked by computer; Barnett writes the same ½), while Gelman, King & Boscardin's state-level `f(.5)/n` has no ½ and is the whole event probability, so published p's can differ by a factor of two on convention alone, which a statistician should check **[I]**. B is a perceived utility difference between two outcomes; with three or more candidates it becomes a sum of pairwise terms; no source states it for a chamber seat. The frameworks sort by what they count as the vote mattering: **pivotality** (an exact fit to the owner's setup; p about 1 in 10 million at best in a US presidential race and about 1 in 60 million for the average voter, with Barnett's `d ≥ 1/N` under two named assumptions the main dissent and the Liron & Enoch reply read only as an abstract that agrees with Barnett in part); **causal contribution** and **expressive** views, which accept that p is tiny and say p is not the test; **evidential** views, which "no one knows" does not exclude unless "held fixed" is read causally; **rule or group** views, which change the unit from one ballot to a rule; and **altruistic** views, which keep the counterfactual but put others' welfare in B, making p × B roughly independent of electorate size, so that choice decides the owner's conclusion (Edlin et al., corrected: at α = 0.1 voting is worthwhile for c below $25). On cost the measured figures are small and variable, and none is a dollar C for a person: the share waiting over 30 minutes was 14% on Election Day and 22% early in 2020 and 6% and 4% in 2022 (the 2022 report's six-minute and four-minute waits have an unclear unit); Stewart's CCES means for 2012 are 13.3 minutes overall (12.0 on Election Day, 17.9 early); Chen et al.'s mean 19.13 minutes is time at the polling place, not time in line; Gibson et al. put New Zealand travel time at NZ$2.30 (US$1.60) per average return trip by car; Cantoni finds 1-3 percentage points of turnout per 0.245 mile in two states; BLS average hourly earnings were $38.00 (all) and $32.75 (production and nonsupervisory) in October 2026, preliminary; the Cost of Voting Index ranks laws and gives no minutes or dollars; and no measured information cost was found. The readers marked 408 ledger entries confirmed, 51 corrected and 25 unread; the corrections are in section 8 and what is still unread, with the reason, in section 9.
