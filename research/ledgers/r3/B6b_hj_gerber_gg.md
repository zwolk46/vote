## C. Task 2: what Hamlin & Jennings say

### C1 How the paper defines expressive behaviour
- First pass said: (not stated)
- Mark: confirmed
- Source: hj_pdf; same URL; section 2, the three numbered "defining aspects" at the end; PDF p. 14 (printed 14)
- Quote: "Behaviour is expressive to the extent that it reflects, wholly or partly, underlying concerns that derive directly from the meaning or symbolic significance of actions or choices themselves, rather than their indirect consequences or consumption benefits."
- Quote: "Expressive behaviour is to be understood relative to an audience, either directly or indirectly, intended or anticipated, and allowing for the fact that an individual may, in at least some circumstances, be their own audience."
- Quote: "Neither instrumental nor expressive concerns should be viewed as definitionally more foundational than the other."
- Quote: "in the purely expressive case, the individual responds only to the meaning of the act/choice, so as to act/choose in a way that maximally expresses the individual"
- Note: quotes 1 to 3 are the three "defining aspects" (1), (2), (3), PDF p. 14. Quote 4 is the "pure" expressive case, PDF p. 6 (printed 6). The paper says the three statements "do not serve to fully characterise expressive motivations ... they serve only to provide a structure" (PDF p. 14). Expressive concerns are "a proper sub-set of the concerns that will be considered in a fully rational analysis of all-things-considered evaluation" (PDF p. 7). Their definition is of the kind of motive (meaning of the act itself), with no functional form.

### C2 Direct against indirect, consumption against expressive benefits
- First pass said: section 3.6 is headed "Expressive and consumption voting" and quotes the SEP's "consumption activity" (report line 178, 180); nothing on how H&J separate the two.
- Mark: confirmed
- Source: hj_pdf; same URL; section 2, second and third paragraphs; quotes 1 and 2 PDF p. 5 (printed 5); quotes 3 to 5 PDF p. 6 (printed 6)
- Quote: "distinguish between direct and indirect accounts of choice/action, where a direct account focuses attention on some property of the choice/act itself as the source of motivation"
- Quote: "the indirect account focuses on some more remote outcome that follows (logically, causally, or probabilistically) from the choice/act"
- Quote: "not from the act, but from its meaning. It is this sub-class of direct benefits that are engaged in expressive accounts of behaviour."
- Quote: "The expressive idea identifies a sub-set of all possible direct, intrinsic or performative benefits."
- Quote: "individuals act/choose in ways that respond only to indirect benefits and direct consumption benefits"
- Note: **H&J keep "consumption benefits" and "expressive benefits" apart**: both are direct, but consumption benefits are "the sheer pleasure of the act" (their example is eating fruit, PDF p. 5-6) and expressive benefits come from "its symbolic or representational aspect" (PDF p. 6). In their "pure instrumental" case the person still responds to "direct consumption benefits" (quote 5). So for H&J a consumption benefit is not an expressive one, and the instrumental case is not outcome-only. The first report's heading "Expressive and consumption voting" and the SEP's "consumption activity" run the two together; H&J would not. The "indirect" account includes outcomes that follow "probabilistically" (quote 2).

### C6 The D-term: does the paper use the term?
- First pass said: (not stated for H&J; the first report treats D via SEP, Geys, Edlin et al., Dowding, report lines 183, 186, 314)
- Mark: confirmed
- Source: hj_pdf; same URL; section 3 opening (PDF p. 15 (printed 15)); section 1 (PDF p. 3 (printed 3))
- Quote: "This approach proposes that voters express their respect for duty through voting."
- Quote: "gets around the paradox of voting without challenging the results of the standard instrumental model in relation to electoral outcomes"

### C7 Audience and secrecy ("no one knows") in Hamlin & Jennings
- First pass said: "The owner's isolation ('no one knows') also removes the *social* part of D (Gerber et al.'s reputational returns). But D is not removed from the person's *own* cost-benefit" (report line 186, marked [I]).
- Mark: confirmed
- Source: hj_pdf; same URL; section 2; quotes 1 and 2 PDF p. 12 (printed 12), quotes 3 and 4 PDF p. 13 (printed 13)
- Quote: "While the act of voting is at least somewhat public, the secrecy of the ballot typically assures us that the content of our vote is private."
- Quote: "One possibility is that the actor may form her own audience; i.e. that expression can, at least sometimes, be self-directed."
- Quote: "it is clear that the relevant audience is oneself"
- Quote: "it is the intended, possible audience that matters in building an explanation of the underlying behaviour, and the fact that there was no actual audience may be neither here nor there"
- Note: this supports only the second sentence of the first report's [I] (D survives in the person's own calculus). H&J's definition (C1, aspect 2) requires an audience, but "an individual may ... be their own audience", the audience can be merely "intended or anticipated" or "possible", and "no actual audience" may not matter. They hold that "no audience at all" would remove the motivating force ("It is difficult to see how the idea of a motivating expression can generate the required motivational force if it is has no possible audience", PDF p. 13, sic) but allow the self as audience. They also give a second route on PDF p. 12: even if a secret vote "cannot in itself count as 'expressing' myself because of a lack of relevant audience", it can be "a precondition for expression" in public arenas, and they then say "the vote is not itself expressive" in that case. So H&J offer two routes by which a secret vote can still carry an expressive motive (self as audience; vote as precondition for later public expression), and concede that on the second the vote itself is not the expressive act. Whether Gerber et al.'s social returns need others to know is a Gerber question (D2/D3), not H&J's.

## Found that the first pass missed

1. **H&J keep consumption benefits and expressive benefits separate**, and count consumption benefits as part of the instrumental case; they treat Riker & Ordeshook's duty as an expressive benefit and do not use the symbol D (C2, C6). The first report groups "expressive and consumption" and lists three views of D; H&J are a fourth.
2. **H&J set aside the classification of costs** ("Nothing significant hangs on this", note 7) and define the "price" of expressive action as instrumental benefit given up, not the cost of voting (M1). The paper gives no support for either placing D inside C or outside it.
3. **H&J say expressive benefits "remain benefits even if the individual faces consequential choice"; what changes with committee size is their relative weight**, in words, with no function (M2, B6). The paper is silent on the tie rule and on causal against evidential "held fixed".
4. **H&J file altruistic voting under both routes**: an outcome route that "may not be sufficient to cause" voting, and a cheap expressive route (M3). The first report's section 3.7 has only the first.
5. **H&J give routes by which a secret vote can still carry an expressive motive**: the actor can be her own audience, an intended or possible audience suffices, and a vote can be a precondition for later public expression (C7). The first report's [I] that "no one knows" removes the social part of D fits Gerber et al.'s reputational returns, but H&J's expressive term is not stated to need others to know.
6. **Brennan & Hamlin (1998), as H&J describe it, is a distance-threshold rule in an expressive domain, with no mention of the chance of being decisive** (C5). The first report's "I could not confirm the payoff structure" can be updated, with the caution that H&J's description is not the 1998 paper.
7. **H&J are "not convinced" of the sports-cheering and dinner-party analogies** that the first report (via the SEP) uses to explain Brennan & Lomasky (C4).
8. **The Greater Good article's Fowler account is an outcome channel** ("the belief that politics matters", "will do a better job") and the article carries no sample, no effect size and no title or year (E4, E5b, E6). It also reports Jankowski's 1994-election study (M8), absent from the first report.
9. **Gerber et al.'s abstract** also compares the social reward for voting with recycling, volunteering and returning library books and ties norms to county turnout; the page's reference list gives the published form of Edlin et al. (*Rationality and Society* 19: 293-314) and a 2013 Gerber et al. paper on ballot-secrecy perceptions (M7).
