- **What this file is.** A second pass over `research/historical-close-races.md` (the first pass, kept as it is). ## 1. Empirical studies of how often an election is decided by one vote

### 1a. Mulligan & Hunter (the study the brief names)

Read: the NBER working paper in full (38 PDF pages, `pdftotext`), the IDEAS, EconPapers, Springer and Crossref records of the published version, and the earlier Harris School working-paper record. Fetched 2026-11-07 00:16-00:19 UTC, all HTTP 200. Page numbers below are the paper's own running head ("Pivotal – N").

- **Citation. CONFIRMED, DOI added.** Public Choice 116(1-2): 31-54, July 2003, **DOI 10.1023/A:1024244329828** (Crossref `api.crossref.org/works/10.1023/a:1024244329828`; Springer page "Published: July 2003 Volume 116, pages 31–54"). NBER Working Paper 8590, "Issue Date November 2001", DOI 10.3386/w8590 (`https://www.nber.org/papers/w8590`, `.../system/files/working_papers/w8590/w8590.pdf`, 520,520 bytes). IDEAS also lists a third version, Harris School Working Paper 0025 (October 2000). The Springer reference list (14 entries) cites Gelman, King & Boscardin 1998, Grofman et al. 1998, Stigler 1986 and Arbuthnot 1710, none of which the NBER paper cites, so the published text is a revised text, not the NBER text. [stated]
- **Abstract headlines. CONFIRMED.** Published (IDEAS, EconPapers, Springer): "One of every 89,000 votes cast in U.S. Congressional elections, and one of 15,000 in state legislator elections, "mattered" in the sense that they were cast for a candidate that tied or won by one vote." NBER (2001): "one of every 100,000 votes cast in U.S. elections, and one of every 15,000 votes cast in state elections, "mattered" in the sense that they were cast for a candidate that officially tied or won by one vote. Very close elections are more rare than the independent binomial model predicts." The October 2000 Harris abstract also says 100,000 and adds "Roughly one of every 30,000 elections with 100,000 votes are decided by one vote. For elections with 5,000 or 20,000 votes, the frequencies are 1/1500 or 1/6000". [stated] The word "officially" is in the 2000 and 2001 abstracts and not in the 2003 one.
- **Datasets. CONFIRMED** (every item). p. 2: "vote counts for elections to the United States House of Representatives for the election years 1898-1992"; "ICPSR study #6311"; "Another 3228 elections were omitted ... leaving 16577 US returns for analysis"; state returns 1968-89, "ICPSR study #8907"; "11,047 are coded as being uncontested, and another 179 are de facto uncontested ... This leaves 40,036 elections"; p. 7 "(with almost one billion votes)"; Table 1 (p. 4) average total votes 111,370 (US), 23,658 (state). The "US" sample is House elections only (Democrat against Republican), so "U.S. elections" in the NBER abstract means those House elections. Arithmetic [measured]: 7 + 3,081 + 140 = 3,228; 51,262 - 11,047 - 179 = 40,036; 40,036 x 23,658 = 947,171,688; 16,577 x 111,370 = 1,846,180,490.
- **Counts. CONFIRMED.** p. 6: "Of the 16577 US returns analyzed, only one (1/16577=0.00006) was decided by a single vote." (footnote 5: "That election was in 1910 for the Representative for New York's 36th congressional district, with the Democratic candidate winning 20685-20684."); "Two others were decided by 4 votes, one by 5, and two by 9 votes." p. 9: "there were no US elections that tied, or were decided by 2, 3, 6, 7, or 8 votes." p. 7: "Of the 40036 STATE elections ... analyzed, two were tied and seven were decided by a single vote (9/40036=0.0002). 61,328 votes were cast in those nine elections, or a 0.00006 share". p. 26: "only two STATE elections tied and only 29 were decided by 1-4 votes". p. 25: unweighted q(1) = (10+2)/56613 = 0.0002 falling to (5+1)/56613 = 0.0001 with the latest counts. [measured] 9/40,036 = 2.25e-4; 1/16,577 = 6.0e-5. Note (not a first-pass error): p. 14 says "only 29 STATE and 3 US elections decided by a margin of 4 or less", but Table 2 sums to 31 for the state sample; the 29 leaves out the two ties. The two state ties are RI 1978 Senate 29 (4110-4110) and NM 1980 House 19 (2327-2327) (Table 3). Independent agreement: Gelman, King & Boscardin (JASA 1998) count "6 were decided by fewer than 10 votes" among 20,597 House elections 1900-92, and Mulligan & Hunter's own count under 10 is 1 + 2 + 1 + 2 = 6 [measured].
- **Definitions. CONFIRMED.** p. 5: "a 'pivot election' as one in which the winner's official and final vote total exceeded the loser's official and final vote total by no more than one vote. A 'pivotal vote' is a vote cast for the winner in a pivot election, or cast for either candidate in a tied election." p. 7: "the probability of a tied election is half of that". p. 8: "p is an estimate of the probability that a randomly chosen ballot is cast in an election decided by zero or one votes." Wording adjustment: the first pass said the estimates "come from a margin-based model, not a bare division of counts". The paper gives both: bare divisions (1/16,577; 9/40,036; the vote shares in footnotes 5-6 and p. 7) and the model-based q(M), p(M). The abstract headlines come from the bare division (below).
- **Size relation. CONFIRMED; one scope note.** p. 26: "the frequency of a pivotal ballot ... is closely approximated by 2/v, which is broadly consistent with a binomial model. Second, smaller U.S. Congressional elections are an interesting exception to the 2/v rule ... Third, 2/v seems to be somewhat less than a reasonably calibrated binomial model would predict". p. 20: "Figure 5 shows the frequency of a pivot election, for elections of size v = 20,000, to be 1/6000, so f(1/2) = (1/6000)(2/3)(20001) ~ 2." **Correction of scope:** the first pass attached the 2/v sentence to "state elections"; in the paper it covers pivotal ballots generally, and the next sentence names small US elections as the exception. **Table 2 headers settled** (p. 14): columns are "elections w/ m <= 4", then q(4), its standard error sigma_q(4), and p(4), all x 10^-4. Medium tertile (9,183 / 14,236 avg / 21,398 votes): 12 elections; q(4) 2.7; sigma 0.9; p(4) 2.4, so the first pass's 2.7e-4 and 2.4e-4 stand. Small tertile (25 / 5,570 / 9,181): 18; 4.5; 1.1; 3.8. Large (21,399 / 51,169 / 388,143): 1; 0.2; 0.2; 0.1. [measured] f(1/2) = (1/6000)(2/3)(20001) = 2.22, rounded by the paper to 2.
- **Recounts and "final" counts. CONFIRMED** (section labels adjusted: the quotes are in IV.A, p. 23-25, and V, p. 26, not III.B). p. 23: "the procedure for determining the winner may be flexible and margin-specific"; "These later counts typically reveal a larger margin of victory than officially recorded – as in four cases shown in the Table – and the official counts are not adjusted when the winner does not change." p. 25: "In one Rhode Island case, another election was held to break the originally 'tied' election, even though according to (our reading of) Rhode Island election law, rerunning the election is not one of the legal options. Exhaustive legal research on the conduct of close elections is beyond the scope of our paper"; "even when the election is over, one cannot know for sure whether or not the outcome hinged on one vote."; "This is a 50% reduction in the unweighted frequency and, since 76% of the votes analyzed in Table 3 were not cast in a pivot election according to the 'latest' numbers, a 76% reduction in the vote-weighted frequency." p. 26: "the frequency of a pivotal ballot would only be 1/v, rather than 2/v". Table 3 (p. 24), official then latest: RI 1978 Senate 29 4110-4110, then 2546-2038 (rerun); NM 1980 House 19 2327-2327, unchanged; NY 1910 House 36 20685-20684, then 20690-20684 ("Another recount broke the tie by one, when an arithmetic error was discovered ... both parties recognize that the election was not that close"); ME 1982 House 69 1387-1386, unchanged; MA 1982 Senate 8 5352-5351, then 5378-5347; RI 1970 House 26 1760-1759, unchanged; WI 1968 House 25 6522-6521, then 6523-6521; MO 1970 House 116 4819-4818, unchanged; ND 1978 Senate 27 2459-2458, then 2448-2454 (other candidate "took the office after subsequent appeals were reversed"); UT 1980 House 44 1931-1930, unchanged. [measured] The paper's 76% reproduces: 1 - 24,444/102,696 = 76.2%.

**Settled at the paper's own text: 1 in 89,000 against 1 in 100,000.**
1. In the NBER body the per-vote US share is computed only in footnotes 5-6 (p. 6-7): 41,369 votes were cast in the one-vote election, "a 0.00002 share of all of the votes in the US elections analyzed"; footnote 6: "20684 of those votes 'did not matter' ... so we are left with a 0.00001 share of all of the votes in U.S. elections that mattered." No table gives the per-vote figure. [stated]
2. [measured] From numbers in the NBER paper: total US votes = 16,577 x 111,370 = 1,846,180,490; the winner's 20,685 ballots in the one-vote race; 20,685 / 1,846,180,490 = 1.12e-5 = **1 in 89,252**. Counting all 41,369 ballots gives 1 in 44,627. So the published abstract's "89,000" is that share to two figures, and the working papers' "100,000" is the same share rounded to one figure (0.00001). **Whether the published body derives 89,000 this way is UNREAD**: Springer answers 200 but the text is "a preview of subscription content", "Buy article PDF USD 39.95"; not bought, no login.
3. [measured] **The two abstract figures are not computed alike.** The state share counts all ballots in the nine pivot elections: 61,328 / 947,171,688 = 6.5e-5 = 1 in 15,444 (the abstract's "15,000"). Under the US treatment (only ballots cast for a candidate who tied or won by one vote: both sides of the two ties, the winner's side in the seven one-vote races, from Table 3's official column) the state figure would be 37,104 ballots, 3.9e-5, 1 in 25,527. Under the state treatment the US figure would be 1 in 44,627. The paper's Table 3 totals for the nine state elections sum to 61,327, one fewer than the text's 61,328 (unexplained in the paper). I choose nothing.
